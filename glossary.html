Education & Early YearsRevisionT Level

Key terms

Glossary

Adult-led activity
An experience planned and directed by the adult towards a specific learning intention, such as a phonics group, a cooking sequence or a planned story. Adult-led does not mean rigid or joyless: the best sessions still respond to children's ideas. The EYFS requires a mix of adult-led and child-initiated experiences. Too much adult-led provision squeezes out play; too little can leave gaps in teaching.
Open related notes
AO1
Assessment objective 1: recall, describe and demonstrate knowledge of the specified content. AO1 questions often use command words such as identify, state, list or outline. Answers should be accurate and use the language of the specification (for example, naming the three characteristics of effective teaching and learning, not a vague sentence about 'good play').
Open related notes
AO2
Assessment objective 2: apply knowledge and understanding to a context, scenario or placement example. AO2 rewards linking a theory, policy or process to the child, setting or situation in the question. A textbook definition with no application cannot reach the top of an AO2 mark band.
Open related notes
AO3
Assessment objective 3: analyse and evaluate information and evidence, making reasoned judgements and drawing conclusions. AO3 answers weigh alternatives (for example, risk-benefit versus eliminating risk), use the scenario's details, and finish with a justified professional decision. Description alone is not analysis.
Open related notes
Attachment
The lasting emotional bond a child forms with a caregiver who is consistently available, sensitive and responsive. Bowlby and Ainsworth described how early attachment shapes later relationships and exploration. In a setting, the key person offers a secondary attachment so the child can feel safe enough to play and learn. Insecure or disorganised attachment is a reason for sensitive practice, not a label to write on a tracker.
Open related notes
Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)
Any method that supplements or replaces spoken language, including Makaton, PECS, objects of reference, communication books, and speech-generating devices. AAC is not a last resort that delays speech; used early it can reduce frustration and support language development. Practitioners must use the child's system consistently, model it themselves, and never confiscate a device as a sanction.
Open related notes
Birth to 5 Matters
Non-statutory guidance produced by the Early Years Coalition as an alternative to Development Matters. It emphasises inclusive practice, self-regulation, and a broad view of progress. Like Development Matters, it does not replace the statutory EYFS. Settings choose guidance that fits their curriculum; examiners expect you to know that both documents are non-statutory and that the EYFS itself is the legal framework.
Open related notes
Characteristics of effective teaching and learning
The three EYFS characteristics: playing and exploring (engagement), active learning (motivation), and creating and thinking critically (thinking). They describe how children learn, not a separate subject. Observation and planning should notice these characteristics as well as the seven areas. A child who keeps trying at a puzzle is showing active learning even if the puzzle is not yet complete.
Open related notes
Child-initiated play
Play in which the child chooses the resources, the direction and the purpose. Adults may still observe, keep children safe, and offer a comment or resource, but they do not take over. Child-initiated play is a statutory expectation of a balanced EYFS curriculum, not a reward for finishing adult work. It is the context in which schematic behaviour and characteristics of effective learning are often most visible.
Open related notes
Co-production
Planning and reviewing support with parents, carers and (where possible) the child, rather than doing it to them. In SEND and early help it means parents help set outcomes, choose strategies and evaluate whether they work. A form sent home for a signature is not co-production. It requires accessible language, time, and genuine willingness to change the plan.
Open related notes
Co-regulation
The process in which a calm, trusted adult lends their own regulation to a child who cannot yet manage a big feeling alone. It includes presence, a soothing voice, naming the feeling, and staying with the child until their body settles. Self-regulation grows out of repeated co-regulation, especially for babies and for children who have experienced trauma. Isolation or 'cry it out' in a setting is not co-regulation.
Open related notes
Continuous provision
The core resources and areas that are always available so children can rehearse, deepen and apply learning independently: blocks, sand, water, mark-making, books, role play, outdoors. It is planned, not leftover. Adults still observe, interact and teach within it. If the same tired resources sit out all term with no enhancement, it is storage, not provision.
Open related notes
Cultural capital
The knowledge, experiences and language that help a child make sense of the world and succeed in education. Ofsted uses the phrase to ask whether settings open doors beyond a child's existing experience without devaluing their home culture. A trip to a forest, a real pineapple, a visit from a grandparent who sews, or a story in home language can all build cultural capital. It is not a middle-class checklist of museums.
Open related notes
Designated safeguarding lead (DSL)
The named person in a setting with lead responsibility for safeguarding and child protection, supported by one or more deputies. The DSL is the first point of contact for concerns, keeps the child protection file, liaises with children's social care and the police, and ensures staff are trained. Every student on placement must know who the DSL is, how to find them, and how to record a concern the same day.
Open related notes
Development Matters
Non-statutory curriculum guidance for the EYFS, published by the Department for Education. It offers checkpoints and examples of how children develop across the seven areas and the characteristics of effective teaching and learning. It is not a tick-list and is not the statutory framework. Settings may use Development Matters, Birth to 5 Matters, or their own curriculum, provided the statutory EYFS requirements are met.
Open related notes
Differentiation
Adapting the aim, resource, adult support, group size or outcome so that children at different stages can access the same opportunity. In early years this is often through the environment and the adult's role rather than three printed worksheets. Differentiation is not the same as separating children permanently by 'ability'. Scaffolding is one tool within differentiation.
Open related notes
Early learning goal (ELG)
The 17 statements in the EYFS statutory framework that define the expected level of development at the end of the Reception year. Practitioners completing the EYFS Profile judge whether a child is meeting the expected level, emerging, or (historically, in older frameworks) exceeding. ELGs are summative; they are not a curriculum to be taught as a checklist from the age of two.
Open related notes
Early Years Foundation Stage (EYFS)
The statutory framework for children from birth to 31 August after their fifth birthday in England. It sets learning and development requirements (seven areas, plus the characteristics of effective teaching and learning) and safeguarding and welfare requirements (including the key person, ratios, safer sleeping, and child protection). It is law for registered providers, not optional guidance.
Open related notes
Eatwell Guide
The UK government's pictorial guide to a healthy balanced diet, used to plan snacks and meals in settings. It groups food into fruit and vegetables, starchy carbohydrates, proteins, dairy or alternatives, and oils and spreads, with foods high in fat, salt and sugar eaten less often. Early years meals must also meet EYFS welfare requirements, allergy procedures and oral-health advice (for example, water or milk to drink, and limiting free sugars).
Open related notes
Education, health and care plan (EHCP)
A statutory document for children and young people aged 0 to 25 whose special educational needs require provision beyond what is normally available. It is issued by the local authority after assessment under the Children and Families Act 2014 and the SEND Code of Practice. The plan names education, health and social care provision and must be reviewed at least annually. Early years settings contribute evidence and deliver the education section.
Open related notes
Enabling environment
The physical and emotional conditions that make play and learning possible: accessible resources, time, space outdoors and indoors, predictable routines, and adults who are interested and available. The EYFS treats the environment as a teacher. An enabling environment is inclusive: a child who cannot reach the sink, see the pictures or understand the timetable is not enabled, however attractive the room looks to adults.
Open related notes
English as an additional language (EAL)
Describes children who are learning English in addition to one or more languages used at home. EAL is not a special educational need. Children typically move through stages from a silent or watching period towards fluent social and then academic English. Home language should be maintained and valued; it is the foundation for additional language, not a barrier to it.
Open related notes
Enhanced provision
A temporary addition or change to continuous provision that follows children's interests, next steps or a seasonal opportunity: a suitcase of postcards after a holiday, extra cylinders because several children are exploring rolling, dual-language books after a new arrival. Enhancement should be intentional and reviewed; piling more plastic on a table is not enhancement.
Open related notes
Family Help
The Working Together 2026 offer that combines targeted early help with Children Act 1989 section 17 (child in need) support. Families get a more seamless service, a consistent practitioner relationship, and a family help plan led by a multi-disciplinary team, rather than being passed between an 'early help' team and a 'child in need' team. It does not replace section 47 child protection where significant harm is suspected.
Open related notes
Forest School
A long-term, child-led outdoor education approach, originally influenced by Scandinavian practice, in which children visit a woodland or natural space regularly, take supported risks, and use real tools under trained supervision. It is not a one-off nature walk. Risk-benefit assessment, waterproof clothing, ratios and a trained leader are part of safe practice. Margaret McMillan and later Forest School pioneers argued that outdoor play is essential for health and learning, not a reward for good weather.
Open related notes
Formative assessment
Assessment for learning: noticing what a child can do now in order to decide what to offer next. It includes observation, questioning, listening and in-the-moment notes. Its value is the change it produces in provision, not the volume of paperwork. A sticky note that alters tomorrow's water play is better formative assessment than a folder of unused photographs.
Open related notes
Fundamental British values (early years)
Democracy, the rule of law, individual liberty, and mutual respect and tolerance, promoted in an age-appropriate way through the EYFS. In a nursery this looks like voting for a story, agreeing rules, choosing play, and encountering different families and faiths with respect. It is not a politics lesson. The Prevent duty expects settings to promote these values and to report genuine concerns via the DSL.
Open related notes
Graduated approach
The SEND Code of Practice cycle of assess, plan, do, review used when a child needs support additional to or different from usual provision. It starts with high-quality teaching and reasonable adjustments, then targeted support, then specialist involvement, and may lead to an EHCP request. The cycle is repeated: each review should change the plan if it is not working. Parents are partners at every stage.
Open related notes
Heuristic play
Elinor Goldschmied's approach in which toddlers explore a collection of everyday objects (tins, spoons, fabric, corks, brushes) with no prescribed outcome. The adult prepares a safe, uncluttered space, sits nearby, and does not direct the play. It supports concentration, sensory investigation and schematic behaviour, and is distinct from a themed craft activity with a model to copy.
Open related notes
Holistic development
The principle that a child's physical, cognitive, language, social and emotional development are interconnected, so a change in one area affects the others. A hearing difficulty will affect language and then social play; hunger will affect behaviour and learning. Planning, observation and safeguarding all need a whole-child view rather than isolated checklists.
Open related notes
Home language / dual-language practice
Using and honouring the language or languages a child hears at home, alongside English. Dual-language books, key words from families, and staff who can greet in home language support identity and cognition. Forbidding home language in a setting can harm attachment, self-concept and progress. Where no adult shares the language, visual support and family partnership still keep it present.
Open related notes
In-the-moment planning
A planning approach in which adults observe what a child is deeply engaged in right now, join or resource that interest, and record the teaching afterwards. It sits alongside (not instead of) a long-term curriculum. Used well, it captures schematic play and spontaneous mathematics. Used badly, it becomes a reason to have no curriculum and no review of who is being missed.
Open related notes
Inclusive practice
Arranging the environment, curriculum and adult responses so that every child belongs and can participate, rather than being present but excluded. Inclusion is different from integration (the child must fit the existing provision) and from equality as sameness (every child gets the identical experience). Reasonable adjustments, anti-discriminatory language and partnership with families are day-to-day inclusion, not a special event.
Open related notes
Information sharing
The professional process of passing on information about a child so that the right people can protect and support them. UK GDPR still applies, but the Data Protection Act is not a barrier to sharing where a child is at risk. Government information-sharing advice asks whether sharing is necessary, proportionate, relevant, adequate, accurate, timely and secure. Record what was shared, with whom, and why.
Open related notes
Keeping Children Safe in Education (KCSIE)
Statutory guidance for schools and colleges in England on safeguarding children. The current version is KCSIE 2026 (in force 1 September 2026). All staff must read Part 1 in full. Early years settings that are not schools follow the EYFS safeguarding requirements and Working Together, but KCSIE is the reference point for schools, including Reception and Year 1, and is widely used as the professional standard for safer recruitment, allegations, the DSL, online safety (including AI-generated harm) and prejudice-based harm.
Open related notes
Key person
The named practitioner, required by the EYFS, who forms a genuine attachment with a child, settles them, builds the relationship with parents, and takes the lead on care, observation and planning for that child. The role is not an administrative label. Secondary attachments with other familiar adults protect the child when the key person is absent. Students may support a key person but should not be given sole key-person responsibility.
Open related notes
Local authority designated officer (LADO)
The officer in the local authority who co-ordinates the response when there is an allegation that a person who works or volunteers with children has harmed a child, may pose a risk, or may have committed a criminal offence against a child. Staff do not contact the LADO themselves as a first step; they report to the DSL or manager, who follows the allegations procedure. Students must know the role exists and never investigate an allegation themselves.
Open related notes
Looked-after child
A child who is in the care of the local authority, for example living with foster carers or in residential care. They have a social worker, a care plan and often a personal education plan. Transitions, loss and trauma are common. Settings should offer a consistent key person, careful handover, and no public discussion of care status. Curiosity about the child's history must never become gossip or graphic questioning.
Open related notes
Makaton
A language programme that uses signs, symbols and speech together to support communication. Signs are used in spoken word order and are always accompanied by speech. Many early years settings use a core vocabulary (more, finished, drink, help) with all children, not only those with identified SEND, because it supports inclusion and early language. Consistency across the team and with families matters more than a large vocabulary.
Open related notes
Metacognition
The ability to think about one's own thinking: planning how to approach a task, monitoring whether it is working, and evaluating afterwards. In early years this is embryonic: a child who says 'I need a bigger block' or 'that didn't work, I'll try again' is beginning to regulate their learning. Adults support it by modelling thinking aloud, using process praise, and giving time to review what happened.
Open related notes
Modes of representation (Bruner)
Bruner's three ways of encoding knowledge: enactive (through doing and physical action), iconic (through images and visual models) and symbolic (through language, numbers and other symbols). Primary mathematics' concrete–pictorial–abstract sequence is a classroom application. Spec v2.0 (April 2026) uses this term, not the older 'modes of development'.
Open related notes
Multi-Agency Safeguarding Arrangements (MASA)
The local partnership of the three statutory safeguarding partners — the local authority, the police and health — that leads and holds to account the area's safeguarding work under Working Together 2026. Education and early years are relevant agencies: they contribute information, follow local procedures and must know how to refer. Annual reports should show impact on children and families, including analysis of disproportionality and racism.
Open related notes
Need-to-know
The principle that confidential information about a child or family is shared only with people who need it to do their job safely and well. The cook may need an allergy; the whole staff room does not need a family's housing history. Need-to-know is tighter than 'everyone who works here' and wider than 'only me'. It is the practical expression of confidentiality in a team.
Open related notes
Next steps
The specific, realistic learning or care focus that follows from observation and assessment. A useful next step is small, observable and can be offered through play, routines or a planned activity ('offer posting tubes and balls to feed the trajectory schema'). A vague wish ('improve communication') is not a next step. Next steps should be shared with the team and, where appropriate, with parents, then reviewed.
Open related notes
Notifiable disease
A disease that registered medical practitioners must report to public health authorities, such as measles, whooping cough or food poisoning of certain types. Settings follow UK Health Security Agency and local health protection advice on exclusion, deep cleaning and informing parents. Students do not diagnose. They follow the infection-control policy, keep confidentiality about which child is ill, and tell the manager if they themselves have a relevant illness.
Open related notes
Observation
Watching, listening and recording what a child does, says and seems to feel, in order to understand their development, interests and needs. Methods include narrative, snapshot, time sample, tracking and learning stories. Records must be factual, dated and stored securely. Observation without analysis is just watching; analysis without next steps is just a folder.
Open related notes
Oral health
The EYFS requires providers to promote good oral health. In practice this means water or milk to drink, limiting free sugars and grazing on sweet snacks, not using food as a constant reward, and sharing NHS advice with families about brushing. Settings do not usually brush teeth unless it is part of an agreed care plan. Dummy use in toddlers can affect speech sounds and is a partnership conversation, not a shame exercise.
Open related notes
PACE (Playfulness, Acceptance, Curiosity, Empathy)
Dan Hughes' attitude for connecting with children, widely used in trauma-informed and attachment-aware settings. Playfulness keeps interaction light enough to reduce shame; acceptance holds the child without endorsing harmful behaviour; curiosity wonders aloud about feelings rather than interrogating; empathy sits with the feeling. PACE is a stance, not a script, and it does not replace safeguarding procedures.
Open related notes
Physical activity and nutrition co-ordinator (PANCo)
A named practitioner who champions healthy eating, oral health and physical activity across an early years setting. The role is not statutory in the same way as the DSL or SENDCo, but many settings use it to keep Eatwell guidance, snack policy, movement and outdoor time on the team's agenda. Students should know who holds the role and how food, allergy and activity decisions are made.
Open related notes
Play Safety Forum
A UK group of play and safety organisations that published the position that children need challenge and uncertainty in play, and that the goal of safety work is not the removal of all risk. Their risk-benefit approach is the reference point for early years outdoor and adventurous play. Citing 'health and safety' as a reason to ban climbing, sticks or messy play usually contradicts this guidance.
Open related notes
Portage
A home-visiting educational service for pre-school children with additional needs and their families. Portage workers help parents become the child's first educators through play-based teaching in the home, then share strategies with the setting. When a child attends nursery, the key person should ask what is working at home and use it, rather than inventing a parallel programme.
Open related notes
Position of trust
A legal and professional concept, defined in the Sexual Offences Act 2003 and later amendments, that an adult whose role gives them power over a child must not abuse that power. In early years every staff member, student and volunteer occupies a position of trust. It covers sexual, emotional and financial exploitation, grooming, and any use of influence for personal gain. Breach is a safeguarding matter, not a private mistake.
Open related notes
Prevent duty
A legal duty on specified authorities, including schools and registered early years providers, to have due regard to the need to prevent people from being drawn into terrorism. In early years this is age-appropriate: promoting British values as they appear in the EYFS (democracy as taking turns, mutual respect, tolerance), noticing concerning behaviour or language, and reporting via the DSL. It is not about spying on families or shutting down children's imaginative play about 'goodies and baddies'.
Open related notes
Prime areas of learning
The three EYFS areas that are foundational: communication and language; physical development; and personal, social and emotional development. They develop first and continue to underpin the specific areas. For babies and toddlers, planning should weight the prime areas heavily. Delay in a prime area (for example, language) will affect every other area if it is not addressed.
Open related notes
Professional love
Jools Page's term for the genuine care, warmth and physical comfort that babies and young children need from practitioners, held inside professional boundaries. It is not a romantic or parental claim on the child, and it is not cold detachment. Professional love means the child is soothed, held and delighted in, while records, ratios, safer working and handover to parents remain intact.
Open related notes
Quality of written communication (QWC)
An assessment requirement in the T Level core examination and some occupational specialism tasks. Marks are available for organising ideas, using accurate terminology, and writing with correct spelling, punctuation and grammar. QWC is not extra polish: a muddled answer that the examiner cannot follow will lose marks even if the knowledge is present. Plan, paragraph, and check names of legislation and processes.
Open related notes
Risk-benefit assessment
An approach, promoted by the Play Safety Forum, that weighs the developmental benefits of an experience (confidence, physical skill, judgement) against the likelihood and severity of harm. The aim is not zero risk. Removing all challenge produces poor physical development and poor risk-judgement later. Controls should be proportionate so that worthwhile play can go ahead.
Open related notes
Safer recruitment
The process of deterring, identifying and rejecting people who would be unsuitable to work with children, including DBS checks, references, identity checks, and questions about safeguarding at interview. Students on placement should have been checked through their provider. Anyone whose behaviour towards children causes concern is a safeguarding matter, whether or not they have a DBS certificate.
Open related notes
Safer sleeping / SIDS
Sudden infant death syndrome is the unexplained death of a baby, often during sleep. NHS safer-sleeping advice includes placing babies on their back, keeping the sleep space clear, using a firm flat surface, avoiding overheating, keeping the cot smoke-free, and not sharing a sleep surface in a setting. Every baby room must follow the setting's safer-sleeping procedure, including checks and records. Students never invent their own sleeping arrangements.
Open related notes
Scaffolding
Temporary, adjustable support that helps a child complete a task they could not yet manage alone, then is faded as competence grows. It might be a physical prompt, a simpler tool, a demonstration, or a well-timed question. Unlike doing the task for the child, scaffolding keeps the child as the active learner. Bruner and colleagues developed the idea from Vygotsky's ZPD.
Open related notes
Schema
A repeated pattern of behaviour through which a child explores an idea, described by Piaget and developed for early years by Chris Athey. Common patterns include trajectory (lines, throwing, dropping), rotation, enclosing, enveloping, transporting, connecting and positioning. Recognising a schema lets adults provide resources that feed the investigation rather than stopping it as 'mess' or 'misbehaviour'.
Open related notes
Secondary attachment
A secure relationship a child forms with a familiar adult who is not their primary caregiver, typically the key person and then other regular staff. Secondary attachments allow the child to cope with separation from parents and with the key person's absence. They are built through repeated, sensitive care, not through being passed around the team. A buddy key person system is how settings plan for this.
Open related notes
Section 17 (child in need)
A duty in the Children Act 1989 requiring the local authority to provide services for a child who is unlikely to achieve or maintain a reasonable standard of health or development without support, or who is disabled. A child in need is not the same as a child at risk of significant harm. Settings may contribute to a child-in-need plan through the Team Around the Child. Information is still shared on a need-to-know basis.
Open related notes
Section 47 (child protection enquiry)
A Children Act 1989 duty requiring the local authority to investigate when it has reasonable cause to suspect that a child is suffering, or is likely to suffer, significant harm. This is the child-protection threshold, higher than section 17. Practitioners do not decide whether section 47 is met; they report concerns to the DSL, who refers if the threshold may be met. Never investigate, interview the child repeatedly, or alert an alleged abuser.
Open related notes
Self-concept
The picture a child holds of themselves, formed from self-image (how I think I am), self-esteem (how I feel about that), and the ideal self (how I wish I were). Adult responses, peer feedback, culture, media and success or failure at tasks all feed self-concept. Practitioners protect it by using realistic expectations, specific praise, and avoiding labels that a child may internalise.
Open related notes
Self-regulation
The developing ability to manage one's own thoughts, feelings and behaviour and to adjust to changing situations. It depends on brain maturation and on repeated co-regulation with adults. A toddler waiting for a turn, a pre-schooler whispering in a library, and a Reception child persisting with a tricky task are all practising it. Hunger, tiredness, trauma and chaotic environments all make self-regulation harder.
Open related notes
Settling in
The planned process by which a child and family become familiar with a setting, usually through staged visits, shorter sessions, and the key person being available at handover. Rushing settling to fill a space increases distress and can damage attachment. Parents' presence, transitional objects, and consistent routines all help. Settling is complete when the child can use the key person as a secure base, not merely when they stop crying.
Open related notes
Special educational needs and disabilities co-ordinator (SENDCo)
The practitioner responsible for co-ordinating SEND provision, the graduated approach, and liaison with parents and specialists. In early years the SENDCo supports colleagues to identify need, plan support, and request further assessment. They do not replace the key person's relationship with the child. Every placement student should know who the SENDCo is and how to raise a developmental concern.
Open related notes
Specific areas of learning
The four EYFS areas that grow out of the prime areas: literacy; mathematics; understanding the world; and expressive arts and design. They should be taught through play and meaningful experience, not as formal lessons for two-year-olds. A child with weak prime-area development will struggle in specific areas; the response is to strengthen the foundations, not only to drill letters and numbers.
Open related notes
Speech and language therapist (SALT)
A registered health professional who assesses and supports speech, language, communication and sometimes swallowing. In early years they may work in clinic, at home or in the setting, and will leave strategies (for example, reduced questions, visual support, specific vocabulary) for staff and parents to use daily. The setting's job is consistent delivery between visits, not waiting for the therapist to 'fix' the child in a weekly slot.
Open related notes
Summative assessment
Assessment of learning at a point in time: the two-year progress check, the Reception Baseline Assessment, the EYFS Profile, or a setting's own review. It should be based on a range of evidence, including parents' views, not a single test. Summative information still feeds planning for the next term or the next setting; it is not a full stop.
Open related notes
Sustained shared thinking
Two or more people (adult and child, or children together) working intellectually on a problem, narrative or idea, extending it over time. It is more than closed questioning. The adult contributes ideas, waits, and follows the child's lead. Siraj-Blatchford and colleagues identified it as a feature of high-quality early years practice. It cannot happen if adults only supervise or only instruct.
Open related notes
Team Around the Child (TAC)
A multi-agency meeting and working group focused on one child and family, bringing together the people who actually work with them: key person or teacher, SENDCo, health visitor, social worker, SALT and parents. The aim is one plan, not a stack of unconnected plans. The child's and family's views should be present. Confidentiality is need-to-know within the team, not gossip outside it.
Open related notes
Threshold competence
The level of knowledge, skill and behaviour that employers consider sufficient for a T Level student to enter employment in the occupational specialism and then develop full competence with support. For Early Years Educator it is demonstrated through the synoptic assignments and placement, not through a single test. It means you can practise safely and professionally, not that you have finished learning.
Open related notes
Trauma-informed practice
An approach that asks 'what has happened to this child?' rather than 'what is wrong with this child?', and that prioritises safety, predictability, relationship and co-regulation. It does not require the practitioner to know the details of the trauma, and it never involves asking a child to recount distressing events. PACE, a calm environment, and close partnership with carers and specialists are typical features. Safeguarding duties still apply.
Open related notes
UK GDPR
The UK General Data Protection Regulation, read with the Data Protection Act 2018, governing how personal data is collected, stored, shared and deleted. Children's photographs, learning journals, addresses and health information are personal data. Safeguarding concerns can be shared without consent where necessary to protect a child; that is a lawful basis, not a casual exception. Students must not take images home on personal phones or discuss children in public or on social media.
Open related notes
United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC)
An international treaty setting out children's civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights. Article 12 (the right to express views and have them taken seriously) underpins voice-of-the-child practice. Article 31 protects play and rest. Article 19 protects children from violence. UK early years practice is expected to respect these rights even though the convention is not fully incorporated into domestic law in the same way in every UK nation.
Open related notes
Voice of the child
The practice of finding out and taking seriously a child's views, feelings and preferences, in line with UNCRC Article 12. For babies and non-verbal children, voice is read through behaviour, play, body language and what trusted adults know. It is not a token questionnaire. Voice of the child must influence planning, care routines and safeguarding decisions, while adults still hold responsibility for safety.
Open related notes
Whistleblowing
Raising a concern about malpractice, including a safeguarding failure, when internal reporting has not worked or cannot be used. Settings must have a whistleblowing policy. Staff and students are protected when they raise a genuine concern in the public interest. In safeguarding, if the DSL or manager is the problem, the concern goes to the deputy, the LADO, Ofsted or the NSPCC whistleblowing helpline, not to social media.
Open related notes
Working Together to Safeguard Children
Statutory guidance (current version March 2026) that sets out how agencies in England must work together to protect children, including early years, health, the police and local authorities. It sits alongside the EYFS safeguarding requirements and, in schools, KCSIE. Working Together 2026 introduces Family Help (targeted early help combined with Children Act 1989 s.17 support), Multi-Agency Safeguarding Arrangements (MASA), family group decision-making, and stronger expectations on anti-racist and anti-discriminatory practice. It applies to all children, including kinship care, looked-after children and unborn children where there are concerns.
Open related notes
Zone of proximal development (ZPD)
Vygotsky's idea that the most powerful learning sits between what a child can already do alone and what they can do with skilled help. Provision that stays inside what the child can already do offers no stretch; provision that sits far beyond it produces frustration. Practitioners use observation to find the ZPD, then offer just enough support for the child to succeed and later to do the same thing independently.
