Early years pedagogy is what you do, and why. Pair each theory with a named method and a room example — that is how 2.5 and the Early Years Educator specialism are marked.

Five theories, named early years approaches, and the adult moves you should be able to spot on placement.

  • Theory

    Behaviourism

    Learning is a change in observable behaviour, shaped by what comes before (antecedent) and after (consequence). Association, continuity, and operant conditioning (positive and negative reinforcement) sit here.

  • Theory

    Cognitive constructivism

    Learners build schemas. New information is assimilated into what they already know, or the schema is accommodated when it no longer fits. Stages are sequential and schematic (Piaget).

  • Theory

    Social constructivism

    Learning is an active social process. The quality of interactions, the more knowledgeable other, and the child's home and culture shape what can be understood (Vygotsky; ZPD).

  • Theory

    Connectivism

    Learning is making links between nodes of information — people, sites, databases, AI. Knowing where to find current knowledge matters as much as storing facts. Siemens and Downes.

  • Theory

    Humanism

    The learner is a whole person with emotional and social needs, moving towards self-actualisation and agency (Maslow, Rogers). Pedagogy is student-centred and the climate must feel safe.

  • Named approach

    HighScope

    Active learning through a plan-do-review cycle. Children plan their play, do it, then recall with an adult. Specified as cognitive-constructivist pedagogy.

  • Named approach

    Montessori

    Self-directed activity in a prepared environment. Practical-life and sensorial materials are self-correcting. The adult observes, prepares and offers, then steps back.

  • Named approach

    Heuristic play (Goldschmied)

    Elinor Goldschmied's treasure basket (babies, seated) and heuristic play (mobile toddlers) offer mixed natural and household objects. The child discovers properties; the adult prepares and stays present without demonstrating a 'right' use.

  • Named approach

    Free-flow play (Bruce)

    Tina Bruce argues that free-flow play is first-hand, intrinsically motivated, and uses technical prowess, competence and a wallowing in ideas. Adults resource and protect time; they do not hijack the play with their own outcomes.

  • Named approach

    Spiral of play (Moyles)

    Janet Moyles describes a spiral: free play, then adult-directed playful teaching that extends the learning, then back to enriched free play. Child-initiated and adult-led are not enemies; they feed each other.

  • Named approach

    Forest School

    Long-term, child-led outdoor education in a woodland or natural space, with supported risk and real tools under a trained leader. Margaret McMillan argued outdoor play is for health and learning, not a fair-weather extra.

  • Named approach

    Steiner / Waldorf

    Creativity, rhythm, imagination and expression. Natural materials, delayed formal academics, and a strong daily/seasonal rhythm. Play and the arts are the curriculum in the early years, not a bolt-on.

  • Named approach

    Outdoor play (McMillan)

    Margaret McMillan (and Rachel McMillan) argued that gardens, fresh air and outdoor play are essential for health, development and learning — not a reward for finishing indoor work or for dry weather.

  • In the room

    Characteristics of effective teaching and learning

    The EYFS names how children learn: playing and exploring; active learning; creating and thinking critically. Observe these as carefully as you observe the seven areas.

  • In the room

    Schemas

    Repeated play patterns through which children explore ideas: trajectory, rotation, enclosing, enveloping, transporting, connecting, positioning. Ainsworth is attachment; schemas are cognitive play patterns (often linked to Piaget's action-knowledge).

  • In the room

    Sustained shared thinking

    A high-quality interaction in which adult and child think together, extending an idea over time. It is social-constructivist practice, not a test of closed questions.

  • In the room

    Child-initiated and adult-led

    A balanced EYFS curriculum uses both. Adult-led teaching (phonics, a cooking sequence) should still leave room for children's ideas. Child-initiated play is not leftover time after 'the real work'.
