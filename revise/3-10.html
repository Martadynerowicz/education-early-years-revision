3.10 How abuse, neglect, bullying, persecution and violence can affect development and behaviour

NCFE specification p.51

Core exam · Section B

Core exam

Harm does not stay in one domain. Abuse, neglect, bullying, persecution and violence can damage educational attainment, attachments and relationships, physical health, mental health, and behaviour (including, for some young people, substance misuse). Effects vary and are not a life sentence: later safe relationships, predictable care and the right help can still change the trajectory. Behaviour that looks like defiance is often a survival response.

Must know

  • Educational attainment: chronic fear and missed sessions consume working memory and attention; language and curiosity shrink where neglect reduces serve-and-return talk; attainment gaps widen and school refusal can follow.
  • Attachments and relationships: insecure or disorganised attachment; difficulty trusting adults; indiscriminate affection after privation; struggle to make or keep friends; repeating victim or controlling roles in peer groups.
  • Physical health: injuries, poor growth, tiredness, untreated illness, psychosomatic symptoms (stomach aches, headaches), delayed motor skills where play has been limited.
  • Mental health: anxiety, depression, shame, low self-esteem, post-traumatic responses, self-blame. Practitioners notice and refer; they do not diagnose.
  • Behaviour: withdrawal or aggression; regression (toileting, sleep); hypervigilance; controlling play; risk-taking; school refusal; in adolescence, substance misuse as self-medication or peer exploitation.
  • Bullying and persecution (including racism, homophobia, disablism) produce similar mental health, attendance and attainment effects even when there is no caregiver abuse. KCSIE treats bullying and child-on-child abuse as safeguarding.
  • Domestic abuse and violence: children who see, hear or experience the effects are victims under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021. They may copy roles, freeze, or try to protect siblings. Effects are likely, not certain — avoid labelling the child as the problem.

Impact across the specification domains

  • Attainment falls when a child is in a threat state: attention, memory and the confidence to take learning risks are crowded out. Neglect in the early years also removes the talk and play that build language, so the child presents as 'behind' when the issue is omitted care.
  • Attachments and relationships are the mechanism behind much of the rest. A child who cannot use an adult as a secure base will struggle to play, to be taught and to repair conflict. Bowlby's attachment work and later trauma-informed practice both support putting safety and relationship before curriculum catch-up.
  • Physical and mental health travel together: poor sleep and appetite, medically unexplained pain, anxiety and depression. Behaviour then shows the survival pattern — fight, flight, freeze — which settings may misread as ADHD, 'rudeness' or 'attention-seeking'. Substance misuse in older pupils can be both impact and a further risk.

Bullying, persecution, violence — and what practitioners do

  • Peer bullying and persecution are not 'character-building'. They predict anxiety, depression, absence and falling attainment, and the child who harms others also needs intervention. Persecution related to a protected characteristic engages the Equality Act 2010 as well as safeguarding.
  • Violence in the home or community can normalise aggression or produce frozen watchfulness. Food-hoarding, inability to sit with a back to the door, or hitting at tidy-up may be protection, not attack.
  • Safeguard first (DSL). Then keep the child in ordinary, predictable nursery or school life as far as possible. Co-regulation, a key person, PACE-informed responses, and partnership with family, virtual school or CAMHS where appropriate, support recovery. Punitive approaches that ignore cause re-traumatise; boundaries still matter, delivered without humiliation.

Named theorists

  • John Bowlby / Mary Main

    Insecure or disorganised attachment after frightening or frightened care undermines later relationships, exploration and learning.

  • Bruce Perry (trauma-informed practice)

    Repeated fear shapes regulation and learning; safety and relationship need to come before curriculum catch-up.

Legislation and guidance

  • Domestic Abuse Act (2021)

    Children who see, hear or experience the effects are victims; impact on mental health and behaviour is recognised in law.

  • Children Act (1989)

    Harm includes impairment of health or development; welfare is paramount.

  • Equality Act (2010)

    Persecution related to a protected characteristic is unlawful; settings must act.

  • Keeping Children Safe in Education (2026)

    Bullying, child-on-child abuse and extra-familial harm are safeguarding issues.

Exam moves

  • Cover more than one specification domain (attainment, attachments, physical health, mental health, behaviour) or you cap the marks.
  • Link a specific harm (neglect vs bullying vs domestic abuse) to a specific effect, then a practitioner response.
  • AO3: effects are likely, not certain; later safe relationships can still help; do not write that every abused child will misuse substances.
  • Name the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 if the scenario is violence at home.
