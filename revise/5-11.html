5.11 Building self-regulation and resilience in a setting

NCFE specification p.59

Core exam · Section D

Core exam

Self-regulation is the ability to manage attention, impulses and emotions; resilience is the ability to recover and keep going after setbacks. Neither is a personality trait you either have or lack — both are taught through relationships, practice and a thoughtfully planned environment.

Must know

  • Games and interactive sessions for turn-taking and impulse control (stop/go, board games, waiting for a timer, parachute).
  • Stories for reflection on emotions (naming feelings, talking about characters' choices, social stories).
  • Modelling coping skills: adults narrate 'I'm frustrated, I'm going to breathe and try again'.
  • Physical exercise to discharge arousal and improve mood and sleep.
  • Problem solving: 'what could we try next?' rather than rescuing or punishing only.
  • Socialisation opportunities so children practise recovery with peers, not only with adults.
  • Reframing challenges positively (a growth-mindset commentary: 'you can't do it yet').
  • Mindfulness and calm-down tools (breathing, grounding, quiet corners) taught before they are needed.
  • Supported risks: climbing, performing, trying a harder task with an adult as safety net — this is how resilience is earned.
  • Co-regulation comes first, especially for younger children and those with trauma: the adult lends their calm, then gradually hands regulation over.

Building self-regulation in the setting

  • Impulse control is a muscle. Short, playful practice (freeze games, passing a cup of water, 'first/then') in calm moments transfers better than a lecture after a bite.
  • Stories, emotion coaching ('you wanted the bike; snatching hurt Sam; let's try 'my turn'') and visual supports (feelings fans, 5-point scales) give children a language for states they can then manage.
  • Physical exercise, outdoor play and movement breaks are regulation strategies, not rewards to be withdrawn when behaviour is difficult — withdrawing them often makes regulation harder.
  • Mindfulness is brief and concrete in early years (blowing a feather, listening for a sound) and can be more structured in secondary (timed breathing before an exam). It is not a substitute for changing an overwhelming environment.

Building resilience

  • Resilience grows when a challenge is real but survivable. Supported risks — a new climbing route, a speaking part, a test paper — with an adult who will help the child regroup afterwards, teach 'I can cope'.
  • Reframing ('that went wrong; what did we learn?') and problem solving stop children seeing a setback as proof they are the problem. Dweck’s growth commentary is useful if it is honest, not toxic positivity.
  • Socialisation opportunities (group challenges, clubs, restorative conversations) mean peers become a resilience resource. A child who never gets to repair a friendship does not learn that relationships can recover.
  • Model coping: if staff hide all adult difficulty, children never see repair. If staff panic, children borrow that too. Bandura’s modelling applies to emotional skills as much as to painting.

Named theorists

  • Albert Bandura

    Coping skills are learned by watching others; self-efficacy grows through successful, supported challenges.

  • Carol Dweck

    Reframing challenge as a chance to learn ('yet') supports resilience if feedback is specific and honest.

  • Lev Vygotsky

    Co-regulation is social: adults and peers scaffold emotional control within the zone of proximal development until it becomes self-regulation.

Exam moves

  • Name approaches from the spec list (games, stories, modelling, exercise, problem solving, socialisation, reframing, mindfulness, supported risks) — do not invent a generic 'be kind'.
  • Apply each approach to a setting (carpet, playground, exam hall, forest school).
  • Sequence: co-regulation then self-regulation; supported risk then reflection.
