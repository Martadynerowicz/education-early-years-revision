5.3 How friendships and relationships support wellbeing

NCFE specification p.57

Core exam · Section D

Core exam

Warm, reciprocal friendships and relationships are a protective factor, not a luxury. They support mental health, resilience, social skills, self-esteem and confidence, and a more positive self-concept. Isolation and chronic conflict do the reverse, so practitioners plan for belonging as deliberately as they plan for phonics.

Must know

  • Positive friendships and relationships support mental health: belonging reduces anxiety and low mood; someone to talk to buffers stress.
  • Resilience is strengthened when a child has at least one trusted person and a peer who helps them recover after a setback.
  • Social skills (turn-taking, reading cues, repairing conflict, joining a group) are rehearsed in real friendships, not only in adult-led circle time.
  • Self-esteem and confidence grow when peers choose you, laugh with you and notice what you can do.
  • Self-concept ('I am likeable / I am a good friend / I belong in this class') is built from repeated social evidence.
  • A single trusted adult relationship (key person, tutor, mentor) can offset a difficult peer week; both adult and peer relationships matter.
  • The same mechanisms can harm: exclusion, bullying and controlling friendships damage the same wellbeing factors — exam answers can show this balance.

Mental health, resilience and social skills

  • A child with a playmate at playtime has a reason to come in; attendance and mood often lift together. For adolescents, a trusted friend is frequently the first person they tell about worry, identity or home stress — a protective factor if adults also remain available.
  • Resilience is not grit in isolation. It is being able to try again because someone sits with you after the fall. Group games, shared projects and restorative conversations after a falling-out all build that recovery muscle.
  • Social skills need an audience. Turn-taking, waiting, compromising and reading facial expression are practised in pairs and small groups. Children with SEND or EAL may need the unwritten rules taught explicitly so they can access those wellbeing benefits.

Self-esteem, confidence and self-concept

  • Being chosen for a game, having a birthday invitation or being asked to help a peer is data for self-esteem. Repeated social success ('they wanted me') is more powerful than a sticker from an adult alone.
  • Confidence to speak, try a new club or walk into the dinner hall often follows a friendship, not the other way round. Practitioners who only coach 'be more confident' without structuring buddy systems miss the mechanism.
  • Self-concept in middle childhood becomes comparative. Friendships that value effort, kindness and shared interests protect a child whose academic self-image is shaky. Cliques that value appearance or 'banter' can distort self-concept quickly.

Named theorists

  • John Bowlby

    Early attachment relationships provide the template for later friendships; a secure base supports exploration towards peers.

  • Albert Bandura

    Social learning: children copy the social behaviours they see rewarded in friendship groups; self-efficacy grows through successful social experiences.

  • Robert Selman

    Friendship understanding deepens with age from momentary playmates to intimate, interdependent relationships — wellbeing effects intensify as friendship becomes about trust.

Exam moves

  • Name one wellbeing factor (such as resilience, self-esteem, self-concept, social skills or mental health), then explain how the friendship affects it.
  • Apply to age: a toddler’s parallel playmate is not the same as a Year 10 confidant, but both can protect wellbeing.
  • Stretch: friendships are protective and they can be the vehicle for bullying — show you can weigh both if the command is discuss/evaluate.
