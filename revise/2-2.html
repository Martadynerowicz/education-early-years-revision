2.2 What shapes priorities and debates in education

NCFE specification p.41

Core exam · Section A (with Element 6)

Core exam

What settings prioritise is not only professional judgement. Governmental policy, technology, economics and funding, and the welfare of practitioners and of children and young people all pull leaders in different directions. Exam answers should name a factor, show the debate it creates, and apply it to a nursery, primary or secondary — not write a news essay.

Must know

  • Four main influences to know: government policy, new technology, money and funding, and the wellbeing of staff and learners.
  • Governmental policy makes some practices statutory (EYFS, phonics screening, RSHE, attendance duties) and therefore a leadership priority.
  • Policy debates include academisation, the SEND and alternative provision improvement agenda, funded childcare hours, and how tightly the National Curriculum should specify content.
  • Advancements in technology (devices, platforms, generative AI) change how teachers plan, assess and communicate, and raise debates about the digital divide, screen time and assessment integrity.
  • Economics and funding include the dedicated schools grant, pupil premium, early years entitlements and the high-needs SEND block; real-terms pressure affects staffing, ratios and what interventions a setting can afford.
  • Welfare of practitioners covers recruitment, retention, workload, pay and professional burnout; shortages of level 3 educators and of teachers directly limit what a setting can offer.
  • Welfare of children and young people covers poverty, mental health, attendance and belonging; settings increasingly prioritise breakfast, pastoral support and a senior mental health lead alongside curriculum goals.
  • These factors interact: a funding squeeze plus a new policy plus staff shortages will shape the same debate (for example, whether to keep a nursery's two-year-old offer).

Governmental policy and advancements in technology

  • Policy from the DfE tells settings what they must do. The EYFS statutory framework, National Curriculum, phonics screening check, Reception Baseline Assessment, RSHE guidance and Working together to improve school attendance all create non-negotiable priorities. Leaders then debate how to implement them: for example, whether a prescribed phonics programme raises early reading for all, or whether a knowledge-rich KS3 squeezes arts and play.
  • Recent policy on funded entitlements (expanded hours for working parents) has shifted early years priorities towards places and staffing at the same time as Ofsted still judges quality. In secondary, accountability measures (Progress 8, EBacc) influence option blocks and which pupils are entered for which qualifications. Name the policy and the effect on practice, not a party-political rant.
  • Technology is now a daily tool and a debate. Tablets, learning platforms and AI can speed planning, personalise practice and support SEND access, but they also create a digital divide if families lack devices or data, and they raise safeguarding questions about filtering, monitoring and generative-AI cheating. DfE and Keeping Children Safe in Education expectations on online safety mean a secondary cannot treat 'laptops in every bag' as an unexamined good.

Economics, funding and what settings can actually provide

  • Public money decides ratios, TA hours, specialist support and whether a PVI nursery remains viable. Pupil premium is intended to close gaps for disadvantaged pupils; the high-needs block funds EHCPs. When those budgets are tight, leaders debate whether to cut a reading intervention, increase class size, or reduce the nursery's opening hours.
  • Early years economics are sharp: funded hourly rates, occupancy and the cost of qualified staff determine whether a setting can offer babies as well as funded three-year-olds. A room leader who loses a level 3 educator may no longer meet EYFS ratios, so a funding issue becomes a quality and compliance issue overnight.
  • In a primary, a frozen budget might mean sharing a SENDCo across two schools or losing a speech-and-language intervention. In a secondary, it might mean fewer option subjects at KS4. Exam answers should link the pound to the child's experience, not stop at 'schools are underfunded'.

Welfare of practitioners and of children and young people

  • Practitioner welfare is a workforce debate: pay, workload, Ofsted pressure, and access to CPD all affect who stays. High turnover in a toddler room breaks key-person attachment; a secondary covering lessons with supply staff weakens behaviour routines and subject knowledge. Prioritising wellbeing policies, PPA time and a reasonable marking load is therefore an educational decision, not a luxury.
  • Children's and young people's welfare sits in the same frame. Poverty, food insecurity, poor housing, waiting lists for mental health services and rising persistent absence all change what a setting must put first. A breakfast club, emotion coaching or a senior mental health lead can be as influential on learning as a new scheme of work, because a hungry or anxious child cannot use working memory on phonics or algebra.
  • The debate is about balance. Critics argue that welfare and 'character' programmes displace knowledge; others argue that ignoring welfare wastes the curriculum because children are not ready to learn. A strong answer weighs both: statutory curriculum remains the entitlement, but it only lands if staffing is stable and children are safe, present and regulated.

Legislation and guidance

  • EYFS statutory framework (2024)

    A governmental policy document that sets learning, development and welfare priorities for 0–5 provision.

  • Keeping Children Safe in Education (2026)

    Places online-safety, filtering and monitoring duties on schools and colleges, shaping technology priorities.

  • Education Act (2002)

    Underpins the Secretary of State's power to set curriculum policy that schools must implement.

Exam moves

  • Name one of the four spec factors in the first sentence, then show a debate (for and against, or two competing priorities).
  • Apply to the age and setting: funded hours and ratios for a nursery; pupil premium and phonics for a primary; Progress 8, AI and attendance for a secondary.
  • Analyse means link factor to priority to impact on children or staff — a list of news headlines is AO1 at best.
  • Show interaction where you can: policy without funding, or technology without practitioner welfare, will not deliver the intended improvement.
