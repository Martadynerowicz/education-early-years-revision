2.11 Communication, social and emotional needs of learners with English as an additional language (EAL)

NCFE specification p.46

Core exam · Section A (with Element 6)

Core exam

EAL is not a special educational need, but it does create communication, social and emotional needs that settings must plan for. Learners may need more support with written than spoken English, help to understand the curriculum and to access English-language resources, time to respond, a setting that understands culture, language, ethnicity and religion, and support from peers and family. Unmet, those needs look like low ability, poor behaviour or isolation.

Must know

  • Support for written rather than spoken English: writing (especially academic writing) usually lags behind playground talk; spelling, grammar and essay structure need explicit teaching.
  • Understanding the curriculum: command words, abstract concepts and culturally assumed knowledge (for example 'Victorian Christmas') can block subjects that are not 'English'.
  • Accessing resources in English: worksheets, textbooks, websites, exam papers and classroom displays may be unreadable without visuals, pre-teaching or translation of key items.
  • Time for responding to questions: processing two languages takes working memory; wait time, and not jumping in, is a communication need.
  • Wider understanding of culture, language, ethnicity and religion among educators and peers reduces misreading of behaviour and supports belonging.
  • Support from peers and family protects wellbeing and provides models and advocates; without it, EAL learners are at higher risk of social isolation and low self-concept.
  • Needs change by stage (2.9): pre-production is mostly receptive and emotional safety; intermediate fluency is often written CALP and exam language.

Communication needs: talk, writing, curriculum and resources

  • Spoken English for social purposes often outruns written English. A Year 6 pupil who chats fluently may still under-write in a SATs reading paper because decoding, speed and inferred meaning in a dense English text are harder than conversation. Secondary coursework and GCSEs raise the bar again: nominalisation, passive voice and subject-specific vocabulary are communication needs, not proof that the student 'can't think'. Support therefore targets writing and academic talk, not only oracy games.
  • Understanding the curriculum is a communication need across every subject. Science investigations, maths word problems and history sources are delivered in English; if the learner cannot access the language of the question, the teacher never sees the knowledge. Resources — textbooks written for monolingual peers, websites, revision guides — may assume idioms and cultural references. Without visuals, glossaries, dual-language key-word banks or a reader in exams, the resource itself becomes the barrier.
  • Time to respond is frequently missed. Teachers who ask a question and take the first fluent hand in two seconds systematically exclude EAL learners, who may still be translating. Wait time, think-pair-share, mini whiteboards and accepting a first answer in home language (then recasting into English) meet the need without lowering the cognitive demand.

Social and emotional needs, culture and belonging

  • Arriving in a new language is socially exposing. Children in pre-production cannot easily join jokes or defend themselves in the playground; they may shadow one adult or one peer, or be misread as withdrawn, rude or 'autistic' when the need is language and belonging. Explicit peer support, a buddy who shares a language where possible, and staff who watch unstructured times are part of meeting social need.
  • Emotional needs include frustration, fatigue (listening in a second language is tiring), loss of the competent self they had in their previous school, and sometimes trauma or family separation connected to migration. Nurture, a key person or tutor, and the chance to express feelings (2.4) matter as much as a vocabulary list. Mental health first aiders and pastoral staff should know that behaviour changes may be language exhaustion, not defiance.
  • Culture, ethnicity, language and religion must be understood by educators and peers. A child fasting, using a different script, avoiding certain foods, or standing closer or further away when they talk is not a behaviour target. Mispronouncing names, ignoring festivals, or treating home language as a problem (2.10) creates an emotional need the setting itself has caused. The Equality Act 2010 requires more than a multicultural display: it requires informed, respectful practice so that identity is not the price of learning English.

Peers, family and getting the need right

  • Peer support gives models of classroom English and a social way in; family support gives the conceptual and emotional base in home language. Both need the setting to communicate accessibly — translated essentials, interpreters for high-stakes meetings, not a child used as the interpreter for a safeguarding conversation. When family partnership is weak, the child's needs widen because nobody is bridging the two worlds.
  • Do not collapse EAL into SEND. Some children have both; most do not. The SENDCo and the EAL lead (or specialist, 2.12) should not be treated as the same person doing the same job. A Year 9 student with strong first-language literacy who is quiet in English has a communication and social need, not automatically a cognition need.
  • Exam answers should name a communication need and a social or emotional need, apply the stage and the subject, and say what happens if the need is ignored (under-achievement, isolation, misidentification). Keep strategies light here unless the question also pulls in 2.12.

Legislation and guidance

  • Equality Act (2010)

    Protects pupils from discrimination related to race and religion or belief; language and culture are part of that duty.

  • SEND Code of Practice (2015)

    EAL is not in itself SEND; lack of English should not be equated with a special educational need.

Exam moves

  • Use the spec list as headings: written vs spoken, curriculum, resources, wait time, cultural understanding, peers and family.
  • Apply the language stage from 2.9 so you do not give a silent new arrival a Year 10 essay need, or a fluent speaker only a buddy.
  • State that EAL is not automatically SEND — a common AO3 discriminator.
  • Show the risk of unmet need: misidentification, isolation, or under-achievement in subjects other than English.
