5.8 Behaviour policies and procedures, and strategies for setting expectations

NCFE specification p.58

Core exam · Section D

Core exam

A behaviour policy makes the expected standard visible for staff, children and young people, and parents/carers. Followed consistently, it is fair, safe and motivating. Strategies then turn the policy into daily practice: structure, age-appropriate boundaries, modelling, positive reinforcement, feedback, reflection, co-regulation and relationships.

Must know

  • Importance: clarifies the expected standard for staff, CYP and parents/carers so everyone knows the rules of the community.
  • CYP input and ownership make rules more likely to be followed; tokenistic 'class rules' that children did not shape are weaker.
  • Expectations must be realistic for age and stage (and adjusted for SEND).
  • Fairness and a consistent approach stop children being at the mercy of adult mood and stop them testing every new adult.
  • Safety: policy covers harmful behaviour, bullying, child-on-child abuse and, where relevant, reasonable force — staff do not improvise.
  • Celebrate and reflect on success; this reinforces motivation for attainment, not only compliance.
  • Strategies: structured approach; age/stage-appropriate boundaries; modelling; positive reinforcement; formative feedback; self-reflection; mistakes as learning; co-regulation then self-regulation; positive relationships and communication; clarifying expectations ongoing; opportunities to test and stretch skills.

Why the policy matters

  • Written policy is how a setting shows Ofsted, governors and families that behaviour is not left to chance. It should align with the EYFS or the school’s behaviour policy, KCSIE (behaviour can be a safeguarding indicator) and the Equality Act 2010 (reasonable adjustments).
  • Consistency is fairness. If one TA laughs off swearing and another issues a sanction, children learn that rules are about adult personality. New and supply staff need an induction into the actual procedures (scripts, recording, when to call the DSL).
  • Ownership: children who help draft playground rules understand them and will remind peers. Parents who have had the policy explained at induction are partners, not opponents, when a difficult conversation comes.
  • Motivation: catching children getting it right, reviewing success in assemblies or key-person time, and linking behaviour to learning goals ('we can do science when equipment is safe') beats a list of punishments.

Strategies that set and encourage expectations

  • Structure: visual timetables, predictable transitions, seating, calm corners. Chaos manufactures behaviour incidents.
  • Boundaries that match the stage: a two-year-old needs distraction and a short 'stop, it hurts'; a Year 9 needs a clear consequence they have already heard about, plus a chance to repair.
  • Modelling and positive reinforcement: adults use the tone they want back; specific praise and earned privileges beat vague 'good boy'. Formative feedback tells the child what to do next time.
  • Mistakes as learning, self-reflection, and co-regulation: after a rupture, the adult lends their calm, then helps the child name the trigger and plan a different action. Relationships and ongoing clarification of expectations make stretch possible — children will take a supported risk in a skill (a speaking part, a match, a debate) if they trust the adult response to error.

Named theorists

  • B. F. Skinner

    Operant conditioning: behaviour followed by reinforcement is more likely to be repeated — the basis of positive reinforcement strategies.

  • Albert Bandura

    Social learning: children copy modelled behaviour; staff conduct is part of the behaviour policy in action.

Legislation and guidance

  • Equality Act (2010)

    Behaviour policy and sanctions must not discriminate; reasonable adjustments for disability are required.

  • Keeping Children Safe in Education

    Behaviour, bullying and child-on-child abuse are safeguarding issues; policy must join up with the DSL.

  • Education and Inspections Act (2006)

    School staff have a statutory power to discipline pupils; policy should set out how that power is used fairly.

Exam moves

  • Split the answer: why policy (consistency, fairness, safety, ownership, motivation) then which strategies.
  • Always mention age/stage and SEND adjustments or you miss a specification bullet.
  • Apply: what happens if one adult ignores the policy this afternoon?
