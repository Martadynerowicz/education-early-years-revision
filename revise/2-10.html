2.10 How home language shapes a child's learning and development

NCFE specification p.46

Core exam · Section A (with Element 6)

Core exam

Home language is an asset for education and development, not a problem to be removed. It supports overall language understanding, provides a family support network, shapes self-concept and identity, and underpins social interactions and relationships. Practice that bans or shames home language weakens those four supports and can slow, rather than speed, English.

Must know

  • Home language feeds understanding of language overall: concepts, grammar and metalinguistic awareness transfer when the child is allowed to be bilingual, not forced to replace one language with another.
  • Cummins' common underlying proficiency idea: knowledge built in Language 1 can transfer into English once vocabulary is in place (additive bilingualism).
  • Home language sustains the support network: parents, grandparents and siblings can explain, comfort and teach in the language they hold most securely.
  • Self-concept and identity are bound up with the language of home, culture and community; honouring it supports belonging and confidence to take learning risks.
  • Social interactions and relationships at home — stories, jokes, faith, discipline — happen in home language; cutting it off isolates the child from that developmental resource.
  • A deficit view ('don't speak that here') is linked to poorer wellbeing and can delay academic English because anxiety raises the affective filter.
  • Practitioners should find out which languages are spoken, by whom, and to what literacy level, including children who speak a dialect or who have interrupted schooling.

Understanding of language overall

  • A child who already names colours, retells events and argues in Somali, Polish or Urdu has the concepts. English is a new code for knowledge they largely hold. If the nursery only values English, those concepts sit unused and the child looks delayed. If the adult accepts a home-language word, a bilingual book or a parent-recorded story, understanding of language as a system grows — the child notices that two words can mean one thing, which is metalinguistic awareness linked to later reading.
  • Chomsky's LAD is not English-specific: feeding home language keeps the device working. Vygotsky's view adds that higher thinking is mediated by the language the child actually has; denying that language during a tricky task removes the tool they need to reason. Additive bilingualism (keep home language, add English) outperforms subtractive bilingualism (replace home language) on both linguistic and cognitive measures in the long run.
  • There is a practical limit: the curriculum in an English school is delivered in English, so English must be taught explicitly (2.12). The point of 2.10 is that home language is the foundation that teaching builds on, not a rival to be extinguished.

Support network, self-concept and identity

  • Families are more able to support homework, behaviour and emotional regulation in the language they think in. A Year 4 maths method explained by a parent in Portuguese still builds the schema; a parent told 'only English at home' may withdraw from partnership because they cannot help. The support network also includes siblings, supplementary schools and community groups that keep literacy in the home language alive.
  • Identity is at stake. A child's name, stories, faith vocabulary and humour live in home language. When a setting mispronounces names, forbids home-language chat at lunch, or treats the language as a behaviour problem, the child learns that a core part of self is unwelcome. Self-concept then tilts towards shame or towards opposition, both of which harm engagement with education.
  • Positive practice is simple and visible: dual-language displays that are actually used, staff who learn key phrases, inviting a carer to share a rhyme, and high expectations in English without requiring the family to abandon their language. The Equality Act 2010 and the EYFS partnership duty both sit behind this: language is part of ethnicity, culture and, often, religion.

Social interactions and relationships

  • At home, attachment, play and discipline are negotiated in home language. A toddler's first emotion words may not be English; a key person who ignores that cannot co-regulate as well as one who asks the family for the words the child uses for comfort. Sibling talk in home language is a developmental engine — narrative, negotiation, humour — that English-only rules in the playground can interrupt if staff over-police it.
  • Peer relationships in the setting cut both ways. Home-language buddies can settle a new arrival (a 2.12 strategy) and protect identity; they can also, if overused without English models, reduce exposure. The professional judgement is to value the relationship and still plan English-rich interactions, not to split friends as a default.
  • For looked-after children or children whose home language is not the carers' language, the picture is more complex: the 'home' language may be in transition. Practitioners still ask, record and plan, because relationships and identity do not pause for English lessons. Exam answers should take the four spec headings — overall language, support network, identity, social relationships — and show a consequence of supporting versus suppressing home language.

Named theorists

  • Jim Cummins

    Common underlying proficiency — conceptual knowledge transfers across languages in additive bilingualism.

  • Lev Vygotsky

    Language is a cultural tool for thought; removing the child's strongest language from a hard task removes the scaffold for thinking.

  • Noam Chomsky

    The language acquisition device is not specific to English, so home-language input continues to feed language development.

Legislation and guidance

  • Equality Act (2010)

    Protects race (including nationality and ethnic origin); language and cultural identity are part of not discriminating.

  • EYFS statutory framework (2024)

    Requires partnership with parents and an understanding of each child's background, including language.

Exam moves

  • Use the four spec effects as a ready-made 6-mark structure: overall language, support network, identity, social relationships — pick two or three and apply.
  • Write additive, not subtractive: home language plus English, never 'ban it so they learn faster'.
  • Apply to the family in the scenario: who can help with homework, which language comforts the toddler, what peers share.
  • Link forward to 2.11 needs and 2.12 strategies only if the command invites it; this point is the effect of home language itself.
