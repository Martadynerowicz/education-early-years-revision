2.6 Learning theories and how they shape education

NCFE specification p.44

Core exam · Section A (with Element 6)

Core exam

Named theorists explain why particular pedagogies work. Bandura explains role modelling; Piaget explains schematic stages; Pavlov, Watson and Skinner explain conditioning; Bruner explains discovery, modes, scaffolding and the spiral curriculum; Chomsky explains an innate language acquisition device; Vygotsky explains social interaction and the zone of proximal development. Influence on education means a concrete classroom or nursery practice, not a biography.

Must know

  • Bandura (social learning): children learn by observing models; attention, retention, reproduction and motivation, plus vicarious reinforcement, explain role modelling.
  • Piaget (schematic/cognitive): sensorimotor (0–2), preoperational (2–7), concrete operational (7–11), formal operational (11+); schemas change by assimilation and accommodation.
  • Pavlov (classical conditioning): association between a neutral stimulus and a response. Watson applied behaviourism to children; Skinner (operant conditioning) emphasised reinforcement and consequences.
  • Bruner (discovery learning): three modes of representation — enactive, iconic and symbolic; scaffolding; spiral curriculum — the same idea revisited with increasing demand. (The v2.0 spec uses 'modes of representation', not 'modes of development'.)
  • Chomsky: a language acquisition device (LAD) and universal grammar — children are biologically prepared to acquire language, not only by imitation.
  • Vygotsky: learning is shaped by social interactions and culture; the zone of proximal development (ZPD) is what a child can do with a more knowledgeable other.
  • Influence means practice: modelling calm talk (Bandura), CPA maths (Bruner), talk partners in the ZPD (Vygotsky), not naming the theory and stopping.

Social learning and behavioural conditioning

  • Bandura's Bobo-doll work showed that children imitate aggression they have observed, especially if the model is rewarded. In a nursery, staff who narrate sharing ('you waited, now you have a turn') become models; in a secondary, a peer mentor who talks through revision is a social model. Vicarious reinforcement — seeing someone else praised — can spread a behaviour without every child being rewarded directly. Influence: planned role modelling of oracy, behaviour for learning and online conduct.
  • Pavlov's classical conditioning pairs a stimulus with a response (a bell associated with food). In settings, a tidy-up song that always precedes stopping play can itself settle children because it has been associated with the transition. Watson argued that environments shape behaviour; the ethical lesson is that adults should not create fear associations (shouting, public humiliation).
  • Skinner's operant conditioning is the more common classroom legacy: positive reinforcement (specific praise, house points used carefully), negative reinforcement (removal of a disliked extra drill once work is complete), and extinction (not feeding low-level attention-seeking). Influence: consistent behaviour policies, but not bribery that ignores unmet need. A sticker chart that shames a child with SEND is a misuse of Skinner, not an application.

Piaget, Bruner and discovery

  • Piaget's stages still influence what adults judge 'age-appropriate'. Sensorimotor infants learn through senses and movement — treasure baskets, not worksheets. Preoperational children (nursery to early KS1) are egocentric, use symbolic play and are not yet reliable at conservation, so practical, visual activity beats abstract number talk alone. Concrete operational pupils (KS2) can classify and reverse operations with apparatus; formal operational adolescents (KS3–4) can hypothesise, which is why GCSE science investigations and debate become possible. Influence: match demand to stage, while remembering that stages are typical, not walls.
  • Bruner accepted construction of knowledge but was less rigid about ages. Enactive (doing), iconic (images) and symbolic (language and symbols) modes underpin the concrete–pictorial–abstract approach in primary mathematics. Scaffolding is temporary help that is withdrawn as competence grows — a writing frame, a worked example, a guided group. The spiral curriculum revisits place value, fractions or historical concepts each year with more demand, which is how National Curriculum sequencing is often designed.
  • Discovery learning, for Bruner, is not leaving children to guess without structure. It is arranging the environment so they can find the pattern, then scaffolding the language to describe it. A poorly resourced 'discover it yourself' lesson without a spiral or a scaffold is not Bruner.

Chomsky, Vygotsky and language in the social world

  • Chomsky argued that children acquire grammar too quickly and too creatively to be explained by imitation and reinforcement alone; a language acquisition device (LAD) prepares them to extract universal grammatical patterns from the talk they hear. Influence: a language-rich environment is not optional extra — it feeds an innate system. It also cautions behaviourist drill: repeating a sentence is not the whole of language development. For EAL, Chomsky supports continued home-language development rather than silencing it, because the LAD is not English-specific.
  • Vygotsky placed language and culture at the centre. What a child can do independently is their actual level; what they can do with a more knowledgeable other is the ZPD — the only place teaching has traction. Influence: guided writing, talk partners, sustained shared thinking, worked examples just above current performance, and careful TA deployment so adults do not do the task for the child (which removes the ZPD).
  • Taken together, these perspectives influence different parts of a day. A teacher might model (Bandura), scaffold a problem just above independent level (Bruner and Vygotsky), use apparatus because pupils are still concrete (Piaget), and flood the room with talk because of Chomsky. The exam wants the named idea and the practice it justifies.

Named theorists

  • Albert Bandura

    Social learning theory — observational learning and role modelling, including vicarious reinforcement.

  • Jean Piaget

    Four sequential stages of cognitive development and the construction of schemas.

  • Ivan Pavlov / John B. Watson / B. F. Skinner

    Classical and operant conditioning — association, reinforcement and the shaping of observable behaviour.

  • Jerome Bruner

    Discovery learning, three modes of representation, scaffolding and the spiral curriculum.

  • Noam Chomsky

    Language acquisition device and universal grammar — nativist account of language.

  • Lev Vygotsky

    Social interactions, cultural tools and the zone of proximal development.

Exam moves

  • Name the theorist, the key term (ZPD, LAD, spiral, vicarious reinforcement, conservation) and one applied example.
  • Do not muddle Piaget's stages with Vygotsky's ZPD — one is a typical sequence, the other is a social space for teaching.
  • If the question says 'influence on education', spend more words on the practice than on the biography.
  • Match the age: sensorimotor to babies; formal operations and hypothesising to KS3–4; scaffolding at any age.
