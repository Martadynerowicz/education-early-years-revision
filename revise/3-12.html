3.12 Fair expectations and avoiding labels

NCFE specification p.51

Core exam · Section B

Core exam

Labels stick. Calling a child 'naughty', 'low ability', 'from a problem family' or 'the SEND one' shapes what adults expect and what the child believes they can be. Appropriate expectations, without stigmatising labels, support independence, motivation and confidence, improve academic outcomes, create a culture of achievement regardless of ability, and remove the burden of guilt or blame from the child and their parents.

Must know

  • Not stigmatised: person-first, needs-first language ('a child with an EHCP', 'a child who is hungry') rather than a master-status label. Stigma reduces belonging and is contrary to the Equality Act 2010 and the SEND Code of Practice.
  • Independence: if adults expect a child to be helpless, they over-help and the child never practises the skill. Appropriate challenge plus scaffolding builds independence.
  • Motivation and confidence: Rosenthal and Jacobson (Pygmalion) showed that teacher expectation can become a self-fulfilling prophecy. High, realistic expectation plus specific praise grows confidence; a 'can't' label shrinks it.
  • Academic outcomes: children live up or down to the pitch of work they are offered. 'Bottom group' language and a narrowed curriculum cap attainment.
  • Culture of achievement regardless of ability: every child is entitled to progress and to have success recognised, including children with SEND, EAL and those who are looked after — not only the children who find learning easy.
  • Removes burden of guilt and blame on the individual or parents: poverty, SEND, trauma and language difference are not moral failings. Blame ('if only they tried', 'those parents don't care') damages partnership and the child's self-concept.
  • Appropriate does not mean no standards. Boundaries and ambitious goals still matter; they are matched to stage and need, not to a stereotype.

How labels harm, and what 'appropriate' looks like

  • A label is a shortcut that hides the child. 'Challenging' may mean hungry, frightened, autistic, bored or abused. Once the label is in the staffroom, observations become confirmation-bias: adults notice the incidents that fit and miss the progress that does not.
  • Appropriate expectations are informed by developmental stage, the child's prior learning, and any SEND or EAL need — not by postcode, family structure, gender or a sibling's reputation. The EYFS and National Curriculum both assume progress for every child.
  • Independence and confidence grow when the child is offered a task they can succeed at with the right scaffold, then the scaffold is faded. Learned helplessness is an adult-created additional need.

Outcomes, culture and blame

  • Academic outcomes improve when teachers and practitioners refuse a fixed-ability story. Growth-mindset language is useful only if the work is actually accessible; a poster will not undo a permanently low set.
  • A culture of achievement regardless of ability is visible in whose work is displayed, who represents the setting, and whether children with SEND are included in trips as a planning default.
  • Guilt and blame land on children ('you're lazy') and parents ('they don't read at home') when the real issues may be working hours, literacy, housing or an unmet need. The Children and Families Act 2014 and the SEND Code of Practice expect coproduction, not blame. Partnership (Element 6) starts with curiosity, not a label.

Named theorists

  • Rosenthal and Jacobson

    Pygmalion effect: teacher expectations can become self-fulfilling prophecies for attainment and behaviour.

  • Carol Dweck

    A fixed-ability label undermines a growth mindset; effort and strategy language supports motivation — only if tasks are genuinely accessible.

Legislation and guidance

  • Equality Act (2010)

    Stigmatising treatment linked to a protected characteristic can be discrimination or harassment.

  • SEND Code of Practice 0–25

    Focus on outcomes and coproduction; avoid deficit labelling that lowers expectation.

  • UNCRC (1989)

    Every child has a right to education and to be treated with dignity, not as a problem to be named.

Exam moves

  • Pair 'appropriate expectations' with 'not labelling' — the question wants both.
  • Use a named mechanism: self-fulfilling prophecy, learned helplessness, stigma, blame of parents.
  • Apply to the child in the scenario (SEND, poverty, behaviour, EAL) and say what the adult should say and plan instead.
  • Do not argue for having no standards; appropriate means ambitious and matched, not woolly.
