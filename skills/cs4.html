Core skill

CS4 Spotting and managing risks to yourself and others when planning activities

CS4 is the skill of using a structured risk assessment so that activities remain possible, not cancelled. You identify hazards, decide who might be harmed, evaluate risk, record and implement precautions, then review. In early years this includes children, staff, students, visitors and the practitioner's own safety. Risk-benefit thinking (keeping challenge while reducing harm) is the professional standard, not the elimination of all risk.

Must know

  • The Health and Safety Executive sets out five steps: identify hazards; decide who might be harmed and how; evaluate risks and decide on precautions; record findings and implement them; review and update.
  • A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm. Risk is the likelihood that harm will occur, combined with the severity if it does.
  • Who might be harmed is not only children: include staff, students, pregnant colleagues, visitors, children with SEND, and the public on outings.
  • Precautions should be proportionate. Removing a worthwhile activity is a last resort; adapting resources, ratios, supervision or the environment is the usual response.
  • Risk-benefit assessment, as promoted by the Play Safety Forum, weighs the developmental value of an experience against the likelihood of harm.
  • Recording must be clear, concise and in the setting's format, with enough detail that another adult could run the activity safely.
  • Dynamic risk assessment happens in the moment (a spill, a change in weather, a child's unexpected behaviour) and must still be followed by a record if policy requires it.
  • Your own safety matters: lifting, infection, aggressive behaviour and lone working are CS4 issues, not signs of weakness.
  • Review is mandatory after an incident, a change of group, new equipment, or at the interval the setting specifies.

In practice

  • Before leading a planned activity, complete the setting's risk assessment form using the five HSE steps, and ask your mentor to check that the precautions are realistic.
  • Walk the outdoor area with a colleague and name hazards (uneven ground, water tray, sun exposure) plus who is at risk, including a child with unstable mobility.
  • For a cooking activity, list allergen, scald, hygiene and utensil hazards, then write the control for each rather than cancelling the activity.
  • Practise a dynamic response: if the floor is wet after water play, pause the activity, make the area safe, tell colleagues, and record according to policy.
  • Include yourself on the 'who might be harmed' line when planning a lifting or nappy-changing routine, and use the setting's moving-and-handling method.
  • After a trip or a Forest School session, review the assessment: what nearly went wrong, what worked, and what should change next time.
