Core skill

CS2 Planning and running activities with others to meet learners' needs

CS2 is collaborative practice: you plan and provide with colleagues, parents and other professionals, not as a solo performer. Threshold competence means you can establish professional relationships, share information that others need, contribute to short-, medium- and long-term plans, and help create a positive climate in which activities actually meet identified needs.

Must know

  • Open, confident communication with colleagues and parents includes passing on information that could affect another team, using precise terminology and an appropriate register.
  • Determining a child's specific needs is a joint task: observation, parent knowledge, key-person insight and specialist advice all feed the plan.
  • Collaborative planning covers long-term curriculum coverage, medium-term themes or projects, short-term daily plans, and in-the-moment adjustments.
  • Sharing resources, ideas and best practice is a professional duty, not a favour. Hoarding planning or equipment undermines the team.
  • A positive learning environment is created through high expectations, encouragement and behaviour management that follows the setting's policy.
  • Record-keeping must use correct spelling, punctuation, grammar and precise early years terminology, because other professionals will act on what you write.
  • Simple calculations (ratios, costing of an activity, quantities of snack or resources) sit inside CS2 and must be accurate.
  • Professional relationships are not friendships. Boundaries, confidentiality and need-to-know still apply when you plan together.
  • Parents are partners in planning. Their knowledge of the child at home is evidence, not an optional extra.

In practice

  • Ask to sit in on a weekly planning meeting. Contribute one observation of a child's interest and one suggestion for how the team could follow it.
  • When you notice a child's hearing aid battery is low, tell the key person and the parent at handover using factual language, and record it according to policy.
  • Draft a short activity plan that names the learning intention, resources, adult role, differentiation and a cost or quantity calculation, then ask your mentor to critique the terminology.
  • Offer to prepare a shared resource (for example a visual recipe for dough) that any colleague can use, labelled clearly and stored where the team expects to find it.
  • If you disagree with a planned activity, raise it privately with the planner using a question ('how will this work for Ibrahim who finds sitting still hard?') rather than criticising in front of children.
  • After an activity, add a brief evaluation to the plan so the next adult knows what to change. Evaluation is part of providing, not an afterthought.
