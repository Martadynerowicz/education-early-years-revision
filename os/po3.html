Early Years Educator

PO3 Planning, running and reviewing care, play and learning so children make progress

PO3 is the planning cycle made real: observe, assess, plan, do, review. You plan for individuals and for the cohort; you treat care routines as learning, not as interruptions; you distinguish continuous, enhanced and adult-led provision; and you check whether what you provided actually made a difference. Parents are part of the review, not an audience for a finished display. Differentiation and scaffolding are how plans become progress for every child.

Must know

  • The planning cycle is observe–assess–plan–do–review (also described as plan–do–review). Missing review is the most common reason provision stagnates.
  • Individual next steps and cohort planning both matter. A room can follow a child's schema and still ensure the group meets curriculum intentions over time.
  • Care routines (mealtimes, nappy changing, toileting, sleep, dressing) are pedagogical opportunities for language, independence, maths, PSED and dignity.
  • Consent, dignity and privacy in care are non-negotiable: tell the child what will happen, offer choices, never display a child, never use care as a punishment.
  • Continuous provision is the core offer; enhanced provision follows interest or next steps; adult-led sessions teach something specific. All three should be planned and reviewed.
  • In-the-moment planning captures current interest; it does not replace a long-term curriculum or an eye on who is being missed.
  • Impact is reviewed through observation, children's views, parent comments and whether next steps have moved. Displays are not evidence of progress.
  • Differentiation adapts the aim, resource, grouping or adult role. Scaffolding is temporary help within that, faded as the child succeeds.
  • Indicators of additional need (language delay, limited play, regression) should trigger the graduated approach, not wait-and-see until school.

The planning cycle

  • Observation gathers evidence. Assessment interprets it against typical development, the child's own history, and the curriculum. Planning turns that into provision: an environmental change, a resource, an interaction, a care-routine tweak, or a planned activity. Doing is the provision itself, including flexibility when a better opportunity appears. Review asks whether anyone's learning moved, whose interest was missed, and what to try next.
  • Short-term plans (daily or weekly) should be adjustable. Medium-term plans hold projects and coverage. Long-term plans hold the curriculum, values and seasonal or community events. In-the-moment planning sits inside this structure: the adult who joins Malik's water-transporting and later records the mathematics taught is planning, not avoiding planning.
  • Cohort planning looks at patterns: several children not using 1:1 counting, a group avoiding the outdoor climbing, all the three-year-olds needing more turn-taking. Individual next steps sit on top, not instead.

Care routines as learning

  • A nappy change can include naming body parts respectfully, singing, counting poppers, and waiting for the child to lift their hips. A mealtime can include pouring, choosing, tasting, waiting, and talk. Sleep can include a predictable sequence, a comfort object, and safer-sleeping practice. Dressing can include the language of first/then, left/right, and perseverance.
  • Dignity and consent distinguish education from processing children. Say what you will do. Invite the child to help. Use a private, calm space. Do not discuss the contents of a nappy over the child's head as entertainment. Independence is the aim, but it is scaffolded: you do not leave a two-year-old to fail at the toilet as a character test.
  • For children with SEND or a trauma history, routines may need extra time, the same adult, and visual or object-of-reference cues. That is planning, not indulgence.

Continuous, enhanced and adult-led provision

  • Continuous provision must be sufficient for the curriculum you claim: if you say you teach mathematics through play, the environment must actually afford counting, pattern, shape and measures every day. Adults still teach inside it.
  • Enhancement is precise: extra funnels because of a schema, a real letter after a grandparent's visit, dual-language labels when a new child starts. Adult-led sessions should be short, intentional and evaluated. If the same adult-led craft happens every Friday regardless of observation, it is a habit, not a plan.
  • Differentiation might mean a visual recipe, a lower easel, a pre-teaching of vocabulary, a smaller group, or an adult who scaffolds the first step then steps back. The aim is participation with stretch, not a simplified ghetto at a separate table forever.

Reviewing impact and involving parents

  • Review uses the same evidence as assessment: observations, the child's own comments or play, parent notes, and, where relevant, specialist targets. 'The children enjoyed it' is not an evaluation of progress. Ask: who used it, who avoided it, what did they do that they could not do last week, and what will we change?
  • Parents hold evidence you do not have. A two-minute handover, a photo with a caption, a request ('has she been posting objects at home?') and a co-constructed next step are partnership review. For families who do not come to the gate, a phone call, a translated note, or a time that works for them is part of the plan.
  • If review shows a child is not progressing, escalate through the setting's process: key person, SENDCo, graduated approach, and, with parents, other professionals. Planning that never changes is not planning.

On placement

  • Take one care routine (snack or dressing) and plan a language or independence goal for it; ask your mentor to watch you try it and then review together.
  • Write a next step that can be offered in continuous provision without a new adult-led activity, then check two days later whether the child used it.
  • At planning, ask 'who did we not see in this area last week?' and propose one enhancement aimed at those children.
  • Practise scaffolding: demonstrate once, do it with the child, then stand back. Notice the moment you should have faded the help.
  • Add a parent voice to a review ('Dad said he counted the stairs') so the plan is not setting-only.
  • When you lead an adult-led activity, note two adaptations you actually used and one you would make next time. That is review, not self-criticism.
  • If you see a child struggling in a routine, do not just help them through it; tell the key person so the plan can change.

Assignment-style questions

Explain · 6 marks · AO2

Explain how care routines can be planned to promote independence and dignity for children aged 18–36 months.

Evaluate · 15 marks · AO3

In Reception at Oakfield Primary, Miss Hughes and TA Priya plan weekly. Continuous provision is attractive but has not been changed this half term. Adult-led phonics is daily and strong. Theo (4 years 9 months) can blend sounds but avoids writing and still uses a transporting schema outdoors, filling bags with gravel. Several children are not yet using 1:1 counting. Theo's dad rarely attends stay-and-play and says he is 'not a teacher'. A planning file is full of completed sheets; next steps are copied from last month.

Evaluate how a setting should plan, provide and review opportunities so that both an individual child and the wider cohort make progress. Refer to continuous provision, adult-led teaching and partnership with parents.
