Early Years Educator

PO5 Working with colleagues, families and other professionals to support children's development

PO5 is how the rest of the specialism actually gets done: nobody improves a child's development alone. Partnership is two-way and, at its best, co-produced with families. Information is shared within UK GDPR and, when a child is at risk, despite it. Multi-agency work (health visitor, SALT, portage, social worker, SENDCo) needs a Team Around the Child, not a stack of unconnected plans. Disagreement is handled professionally. Confidentiality is need-to-know, not secrecy that blocks help.

Must know

  • Two-way communication means parents and carers contribute knowledge, not only receive newsletters. Co-production means they help set and review outcomes.
  • UK GDPR governs photographs, journals, addresses and health data. Safeguarding information can be shared without consent where necessary to protect a child; that is lawful, not casual.
  • Need-to-know is the working rule: share what the cook, the key person or the social worker requires to do their job; do not brief the whole staff room.
  • Multi-agency partners commonly include the health visitor, speech and language therapist, portage worker, social worker, educational psychologist, GP, SENDCo and, where relevant, the LADO.
  • The Team Around the Child (or family) brings those people into one plan with the parents. The key person often attends and must prepare accurate, factual updates.
  • Professional disagreement is expected. Use the setting's escalation or resolving-professional-differences process; do not undermine a colleague in front of a parent or a child.
  • Barriers to partnership include shift patterns, language, previous poor experience of services, poverty, distrust, and professional jargon. The setting has to adapt, not blame the family.
  • Confidentiality is a duty to the child. It is not a reason to withhold a safeguarding concern from the DSL.

Two-way communication and co-production

  • Handover, learning journals, phone calls, interpreters, home visits (where used), and parents' evenings are tools. The test is whether information travels both ways. 'How did she sleep?' is as important as 'she ate well'. Co-production in SEND or early help means the parent helps write the outcome ('we want him to sit at family meals without distress') rather than signing a plan written in education-speak.
  • Hard-to-reach is usually a description of the service, not the family. Offer times that match shifts, translated summaries, a quiet space, and a named person. Do not discuss a child at the gate in a crowd. Do not use a child as the messenger for difficult news.
  • The child's voice belongs in the partnership. Even a two-year-old can show who they want involved and what they enjoy. Record it and take it to the meeting.

Information sharing, GDPR and safeguarding override

  • Ask: is sharing necessary, proportionate, relevant, accurate, timely and secure? Record what was shared, with whom, and why. Parents are usually told when information is passed to another service, unless telling them would increase risk to the child.
  • Photographs, observations and medical details do not go on personal phones or personal cloud drives. Students follow the setting's device policy exactly.
  • A colleague who wants 'the full story' out of curiosity is not a lawful recipient. Need-to-know protects dignity and trust. Conversely, refusing to tell the DSL because 'the parent asked me to keep it secret' is a misunderstanding of confidentiality.

Multi-agency working and the TAC

  • Health visitors hold the two-year review and are often the first partner when development or home conditions raise a question. SALT leaves strategies that only work if the room uses them every day. Portage works through the parent in the home; the setting should ask what to mirror. Social workers co-ordinate child-in-need or child-protection plans; the setting's reports must be factual and prompt. The SENDCo joins the threads inside the setting.
  • A TAC meeting needs a clear purpose, the right people, the parent's presence or view, and actions with names and dates. The student may contribute observations but does not lead the meeting. Follow up: a plan that nobody reads is not partnership.
  • Roles can overlap and contradict (a therapist wants a quiet space; the curriculum wants outdoor play). The professional task is to integrate, not to pick a tribe.

Disagreement, boundaries and professional courtesy

  • Disagreeing with a parent about dummy use, diet or behaviour is common. Start with their goal for the child, share the setting's evidence, and look for a trial they can accept. Lecturing at the gate destroys the relationship the child needs you to have.
  • Disagreeing with a colleague (about an assessment, a next step, or whether to refer) should happen away from children, using questions and evidence. If a child's safety is at issue, escalate the same day rather than waiting for a comfortable moment.
  • Professional boundaries with families include not becoming a personal friend, not babysitting on the side without declaration, not using personal social media, and not giving out a personal number unless policy allows a work device. Warmth and partnership do not require those crossings.

On placement

  • Prepare one handover a day that includes a question to the parent, not only a report, and note what you learn.
  • Ask to read (and then contribute to) a report for another professional, checking that every sentence is factual and necessary.
  • Find out how the setting books an interpreter and when it last did so. If a family is receiving everything in English only, raise it with your mentor.
  • Sit in on a planning or TAC discussion if invited, and write a three-bullet summary of actions, owners and dates.
  • When you disagree with a planned next step, practise a professional question: 'How will this work for Aisha given what SALT asked us to do?'
  • Never discuss a child in the staff-room doorway, the car park or a group chat. If you hear it happening, do not add to it; take a concern to the mentor or DSL.
  • Keep a list of who is who (SENDCo, DSL, health visitor contact route) in your placement folder, without storing children's personal data in it.

Explain how a key person should work with a speech and language therapist and a parent to support a child with delayed language.

Discuss · 15 marks · AO3

Amira, aged 2 years 4 months, attends Buttercup Room. Health visitor notes at the two-year review flagged delayed language and poor weight gain. A social worker is completing a child-in-need assessment (section 17). Mum, Yasmin, works nights, misses meetings, and told the student, 'I'm sick of people writing about us.' An interpreter has never been offered, although Yasmin's English is limited. A colleague wants to 'fill everyone in' at staff meeting about the family's housing. SALT has sent a programme that is not yet in use. Yasmin has asked that the social worker not be told Amira arrived hungry twice this week.

Discuss how a setting should work in partnership when a family is reluctant to engage and several agencies are involved. Evaluate how confidentiality and information sharing should be handled.
