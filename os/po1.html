Early Years Educator

PO1 Helping children play, develop and learn in the early years

PO1 is the heart of Early Years Educator practice: using play as the engine of development across the seven EYFS areas, while noticing how children learn (the characteristics of effective teaching and learning) as well as what they learn. You plan a mix of child-initiated and adult-led experiences, recognise schemas and play types, adapt for inclusion, and use observation so that next steps are real. Non-statutory guidance such as Development Matters and Birth to 5 Matters can shape a curriculum; the statutory EYFS is what you must meet.

Must know

  • The seven EYFS areas: three prime (communication and language; physical development; personal, social and emotional development) and four specific (literacy; mathematics; understanding the world; expressive arts and design).
  • The three characteristics of effective teaching and learning: playing and exploring, active learning, and creating and thinking critically.
  • Play types include heuristic play, pretend and socio-dramatic play, constructive play, physical play, and outdoor or Forest School play. Each offers different learning if the environment is resourced and the adult role is right.
  • A balance of adult-led and child-initiated play is a curriculum requirement, not a preference. Adult-led teaching should still leave room for children's ideas.
  • Sustained shared thinking is a high-quality interaction: the adult and child think together, extending an idea over time, rather than firing closed questions.
  • Schemas are repeated play patterns (trajectory, rotation, enclosing, enveloping, transporting, connecting, positioning). Feed the schema with resources; do not only stop it as mess.
  • Observation informs next steps. A next step is small, play-based and reviewable, not a vague wish to 'improve development'.
  • Inclusive play means every child can access the experience: SEND adaptations, dual-language support, and anti-discriminatory resources are part of PO1, not an optional extra.
  • Development Matters and Birth to 5 Matters are non-statutory. They help you interpret typical development; they are not tick-lists and they do not replace the EYFS.
  • Pedagogical approaches in the specification include Moyles (spiral of play), Goldschmied (heuristic play), Bruce (free-flow play), Montessori (self-directed activity), Forest School (risk and challenge), HighScope (active learning), McMillan (outdoor play) and Steiner (creativity and expression).

The seven areas and how children learn

  • Prime areas are foundational. A two-year-old whose language or PSED is insecure will not 'catch up' in literacy by being drilled on letters. Weight planning towards communication, physical development and PSED for the youngest children, and keep those areas visible in Reception too.
  • Specific areas grow out of the prime areas. Mathematics in the EYFS is counting, pattern, shape and spatial thinking in real contexts (snack, blocks, outdoors), not a worksheet culture. Literacy is talk, rhyme, print in the environment, sharing books and, later, systematic synthetic phonics.
  • The characteristics of effective teaching and learning cut across every area. Playing and exploring is about finding out and being willing to have a go. Active learning is concentration, persistence and taking pride in meeting a challenge. Creating and thinking critically is having ideas, making links and choosing ways to do things. Observe these as carefully as you observe ELG-style skills.

Play types and the adult role

  • Heuristic play (Goldschmied) offers treasure-basket or heuristic collections for babies and toddlers: metal, wood, fabric, natural objects, containers. The adult prepares, sits nearby and does not demonstrate a 'right' use. Concentration can last far longer than in an adult-directed craft.
  • Pretend and socio-dramatic play lets children rehearse roles, language and social rules. Resource it with open-ended props (fabric, bags, real or realistic utensils) and be ready to take a role without hijacking the plot. Constructive play (blocks, loose parts) builds spatial thinking, mathematics and collaboration. Physical play, indoors and out, builds the motor foundations for later writing and self-care.
  • Outdoor and Forest School play offers weather, uneven ground, real tools and managed risk. Margaret McMillan's argument still holds: outdoor play is for health and learning, not a fair-weather extra. Adult-led experiences (a planned phonics group, a cooking sequence) sit alongside child-initiated play. The skill is knowing when to step in with a prompt and when to step back.

Schemas, observation and next steps

  • A child throwing objects may be exploring trajectory, not 'being naughty'. A child wrapping dolls in cloth is often enveloping. A child who carries bags of treasures from one area to another is transporting. Name the schema in your observation, then resource it: gutters and balls for trajectory, boxes and lids for enclosing, scarves for enveloping, bags and baskets for transporting, tape and construction for connecting, spinning toys and wheels for rotation.
  • Observation methods (narrative, snapshot, tracking, learning stories) only earn their keep if they change provision. Write what you saw, then interpret separately, then set one next step the team can actually offer tomorrow. Involve the child's interests and the parent's knowledge from home.
  • Sustained shared thinking is the interaction that often follows a good observation: 'You connected all the tubes. I wonder what would happen if this water reached the sand?' Wait. Follow. Do not turn it into a test.

Inclusive play and non-statutory guidance

  • Inclusive play starts with the environment: height of resources, noise, visual clutter, outdoor access, representation in books and dolls, and time for children who need longer. SEND adaptations might include a quieter entry to role play, a visual sequence for a game, a larger grip on a marker, or an adult who models Makaton for the whole group so the child using it is not isolated.
  • For EAL, keep home language visible, use gesture and real objects, and do not mistake a silent period for delay. For children who have experienced trauma, predictability and a known adult matter more than a dazzling new theme table.
  • Development Matters and Birth to 5 Matters can help a team talk about typical development and curriculum design. They are guidance. Do not write 'DM 3-4 years, not yet' as if it were a statutory grade. The EYFS statutory framework, plus your setting's curriculum, is what inspections and assignments will hold you to.

On placement

  • Ask your mentor which play type is weakest in the room this week (often construction or outdoor investigative play) and help resource it for three days, then review who used it and how.
  • Complete one narrative observation that names a possible schema, list three resources that would feed it, and share the idea at planning rather than only filing the observation.
  • Practise sustained shared thinking once a session: comment, wait at least ten seconds, and follow the child's idea instead of switching to your planned question.
  • Audit one area for inclusion (can a child who uses a frame, a child with EAL, and a child who hates noise all use it?) and make one physical change.
  • Learn five Makaton signs the room already uses and model them with speech for all children, not only the child with a plan.
  • When you lead an adult-led activity, build in a child-choice moment (which fruit, which book, which route around the garden) so it does not become a lecture.
  • Read the setting's curriculum or planning guidance and note whether it draws on Development Matters, Birth to 5 Matters, or neither. Be able to say how that choice shows in the room.

Analyse · 15 marks · AO3

Willow Room at Hartfield Nursery has 20 children aged 3–4 years, including Jia, who has a hearing impairment and uses speech with Makaton, and Malik, who spends long periods transporting water in bottles between the bathroom and the sand. The room has plentiful table-top crafts with adult models to copy, a small outdoor area used mainly in dry weather, and group times of 25 minutes on the carpet. Several parents have asked for 'more phonics and less playing'. The room leader wants the T Level student to help rethink provision for the next half term.

Analyse how a setting can support and promote play, development and early education for a group of three-year-olds, including at least one child with SEND. Evaluate the balance of child-initiated and adult-led experiences.
