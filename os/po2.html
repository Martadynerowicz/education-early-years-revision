PO2 Building relationships with children that help them develop

PO2 is about the relationships that make development possible. The EYFS key person is a statutory attachment figure in the setting, not a paperwork title. Settling, secondary attachments, PACE, and careful work with children who have experienced trauma or who are looked after all sit here. Professional love is warmth inside boundaries. The child's voice, including UNCRC Article 12, must influence care. Without a secure relationship, even excellent play provision will not be used well.

Must know

  • The key person is a statutory EYFS role: a named practitioner who forms an attachment with the child, settles them, liaises with parents and leads on that child's care and planning.
  • Attachment in the setting is usually a secondary attachment. It still needs sensitivity, consistency and time; children cannot be endlessly rotated between adults.
  • PACE (Playfulness, Acceptance, Curiosity, Empathy) is a connecting stance, especially useful when behaviour communicates distress.
  • Settling-in should be paced, with parents present as needed, transitional objects welcomed, and the key person available at handover. Stopping crying is not the same as being settled.
  • Secondary attachments and a buddy key person protect the child when the key person is on leave, training or off sick.
  • UNCRC Article 12: children have the right to express their views and have them taken seriously. For babies, behaviour and play are the voice.
  • Professional love (Page) is genuine warmth and physical comfort held within professional boundaries, records and handover to parents. It is not ownership of the child and not cold detachment.
  • Children who have experienced trauma or who are looked after need predictability, a consistent adult, and no pressure to recount events. Trauma-informed practice never replaces safeguarding procedures.
  • Self-concept (self-image, self-esteem, ideal self) is shaped by adult responses. Labels, sarcasm and public comparison damage the relationship and the learning.
  • Co-regulation comes before self-regulation. A dysregulated adult cannot offer a secure base.

The key person and attachment in the setting

  • Bowlby's attachment theory and Ainsworth's observations of sensitivity explain why a child who does not feel safe will not explore. In group care, the key person offers a secure base: greeting the child, doing intimate care, knowing comfort rituals, and being the person a parent can tell about a sleepless night.
  • The role fails when it is only a name on a coat peg. It also fails when a practitioner becomes a rival to the parent. Handover should delight in what the child did and return the child emotionally to the parent. Professional love is the warmth the child needs, bounded by that handover.
  • Secondary attachments are planned, not accidental. A buddy shares enough of the child's care that absence of the key person is a bump, not a cliff. Students support the key person; they should not be given sole responsibility for a child's attachments.

Settling, PACE and the child's voice

  • Settling-in policies should allow staggered starts, shorter days and a parent staying. Distraction has a place once the child has a relationship; it is not a substitute for presence. Separation anxiety is typical, especially in the first year and at later transitions. Respond to the feeling, keep goodbyes honest and brief, and never slip away without saying goodbye.
  • PACE keeps the relationship intact when behaviour is hard. Playfulness can defuse shame. Acceptance holds the child ('I can see this is too much') without accepting harm to others. Curiosity wonders ('I wonder if the goodbye felt too quick') instead of accusing. Empathy sits with the feeling. PACE does not mean there are no boundaries.
  • Voice of the child is UNCRC Article 12 in practice. Ask, watch, and offer choices in care (which flannel, which book, who changes you). Record preferences and act on them. A toddler who arches away from a hug is communicating; that is as valid as a spoken 'no'.

Professional love, boundaries and typical behaviour

  • Young children need to be held, soothed and delighted in. Settings that ban all physical contact in the name of safeguarding often increase distress and can themselves be harmful. Safer working means open rooms, informed consent for care, recording, and no secret special relationships. Students follow the setting's intimate-care and physical-contact policies.
  • Typical behaviour shifts with age: babies seek proximity; toddlers test autonomy; two- and three-year-olds often struggle to wait; older children care more about peers and fairness. Interpreting behaviour through development prevents you from treating a tired two-year-old as 'defiant'.
  • Self-efficacy grows when children are offered real choices, time to try, and praise for effort. Doing everything for a child in the name of kindness blocks both skill and self-concept.

Trauma, looked-after children and transitions

  • Children who have experienced trauma may startle, freeze, control play, or struggle with intimate care and transitions. You do not need the story in order to offer safety. Predictable routines, a consistent adult, warnings before change, and co-regulation are the core offer. Never press a child to talk about home or care history.
  • Looked-after children have a social worker and a care plan. Foster carers need the same quality of handover as any parent, sometimes more, because the child may already have had several placements. Confidentiality about care status is strict: other parents and other children do not need to know.
  • Transitions (starting, moving rooms, starting school, a new baby, family change) can produce clinginess, regression or excitement. Prepare with stories, visits, objects of reference and honest information. Liaise with the receiving key person or teacher so the relationship is handed on, not dropped.

On placement

  • Learn your key children's comfort rituals (dummy, comforter, song, how they like to be held) from the key person and parent, and use them consistently.
  • At every handover, offer one specific delight ('she spent twenty minutes at the dough with Poppy') before any care information, so the relationship with the parent is not only a list of problems.
  • When the key person is off, follow the buddy plan: be available at arrival, do that child's intimate care if trained and permitted, and do not pass the child around the team.
  • Practise PACE in a difficult moment: name the feeling, stay calm, and wait until the child can take a choice, rather than lecturing while they are still flooded.
  • Offer two genuine choices in a care routine (which book at nap, which sink for handwashing) and notice how voice of the child changes co-operation.
  • If a child is looked after or newly arrived from another setting, ask the mentor what the child already finds comforting, and do not ask the child for their history.
  • Notice your own regulation. If you are rushed or irritated, swap a task with a colleague rather than taking a dysregulated mood into a child's care.

Assignment-style questions

Explain · 6 marks · AO2

Explain how the key person can support a two-year-old who is finding settling-in difficult. Refer to attachment in your answer.

Discuss · 15 marks · AO3

Elsie, aged 3 years 8 months, has recently moved to a foster placement and started in Daisies Room (16 children, 3 staff). She watches from the edge of play, startles at sudden noise, and becomes very distressed during nappy changing if her key person, Keisha, is not there. A well-meaning colleague has been asking Elsie questions about 'your old mummy' during snack. Foster carer Dev would like a daily note and a consistent collection routine. Two staff say they are 'keeping a professional distance so she doesn't get confused'.

Discuss how practitioners can develop relationships that facilitate development for children who have experienced disruption, including a looked-after child. Evaluate the balance between professional love and professional boundaries.
