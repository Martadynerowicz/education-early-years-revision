Early Years Educator

PO4 Keeping children safe, healthy and well

PO4 is the non-negotiable duty to keep children safe while still letting them play, eat, sleep, go online in age-appropriate ways, and feel well. KCSIE and Working Together sit with the EYFS welfare requirements. You must know how to recognise possible abuse at a professional level, report to the DSL, understand LADO and whistleblowing, and manage health (safer sleeping, allergies, medication, oral health, infection). Risk-benefit, not the elimination of all risk, is the play-safety standard. Wellbeing includes mental health and transitions.

Must know

  • Working Together to Safeguard Children (2026) and, in schools, Keeping Children Safe in Education (2026) are the core statutory guidance. The EYFS sets safeguarding and welfare requirements for early years providers. Family Help is the 2026 name for the seamless early-help / s.17 offer.
  • The DSL is the first port of call for a concern. Record factually, same day, including the child's words in quotation marks. Do not investigate, promise confidentiality, or alert an alleged abuser.
  • Section 17 (child in need) and section 47 (reasonable cause to suspect significant harm) are different thresholds. You report; you do not decide the section.
  • LADO co-ordinates allegations against people who work with children. Whistleblowing is how you escalate if the DSL or manager is the problem.
  • Signs of abuse at exam level: unexplained injuries, changes in behaviour, sexualised behaviour not in line with age, neglected physical care, excessive watchfulness, a child who is unusually withdrawn or unusually aggressive. Record and report; do not diagnose a type of abuse as fact.
  • Risk-benefit (Play Safety Forum) keeps challenge in play. Identify hazards, who might be harmed, controls, record, review (HSE five steps).
  • Safer sleeping: back to sleep, clear cot, firm flat surface, appropriate temperature, no smoking, regular checks. Follow NHS and setting policy.
  • Allergies, medication, oral health and the Eatwell Guide are welfare duties. Infection control includes hygiene, exclusion periods and notifiable diseases via the manager and public health advice.
  • Online safety in early years includes setting devices, parent partnerships about what children see at home, and no staff or student use of personal phones with children.
  • Wellbeing covers mental health, co-regulation, and support through transitions. A child who is 'no trouble' can still be struggling.

Statutory guidance, reporting and allegations

  • If you are worried, tell the DSL the same day. Write what you saw or heard, when, and who else was there. Use the child's words. Opinion can be labelled as opinion ('I was concerned because…') but the record should be usable in a later enquiry. The DSL may take advice from children's social care. You may be asked for your record; you will not usually be told everything that happens next.
  • If a child discloses, listen, stay calm, do not ask leading questions, explain that you will need to tell the DSL so they can be helped, and do not offer a promise you cannot keep. Comfort the child. Then report.
  • Allegations about an adult (including a student) go through the allegations procedure, which includes the LADO. Do not privately 'warn' the person or start your own investigation. If the person you would report to is the subject of the concern, use the deputy, the LADO contact in the policy, Ofsted, or the NSPCC whistleblowing helpline.

Health, infection and daily welfare

  • Safer sleeping is a daily practice, not a poster. Cots are clear; babies are placed on their backs; checks are recorded; any baby who has rolled is still considered within the policy the setting has written. Never prop a bottle or put a baby to sleep on a play mat as a workaround.
  • Allergy and medication procedures are exact: labelled food, trained staff, care plans, permission, recording. A student must not administer medication unless the setting and provider have agreed it and you have been trained. Oral health means water or milk, limited free sugars, and partnership with families, not shaming a parent about a dummy at the gate.
  • Infection control is handwashing, cleaning, ventilation, and exclusion when policy says so. Notifiable diseases are reported by clinicians; your job is to tell the manager, follow UKHSA advice that comes back, and protect confidentiality about which child is ill.

Risk-benefit, accidents and online safety

  • Children need to climb, use tools, and experience uncertainty. A good risk assessment makes that possible: surfacing, ratios, teaching safe use, weather plans. Cancelling outdoor play as a default is a wellbeing issue as well as a play issue.
  • When an accident happens: first aid, inform the parent, record, review the assessment if the hazard should have been controlled. Head injuries always go to the parent and follow policy even if the child 'seems fine'.
  • Online safety for under-fives is mostly about adults: setting tablets locked to chosen apps, no YouTube autoplay, no personal devices, and conversations with parents about what children see on family phones. If a child describes sexual or violent content, record and tell the DSL; it may be a safeguarding issue at home as well as an e-safety teaching moment.

Abuse indicators, Prevent, wellbeing and transitions

  • You are not asked to become a detective. You are asked to notice a child whose presentation changes, whose explanations of injury do not fit, who seems afraid of a person, or whose behaviour in play is significantly out of line with age. Patterns matter as much as single events. Female genital mutilation, forced marriage and fabricated illness are also in the professional landscape for older children; in early years your duty is still to report concerns, not to investigate.
  • Prevent in the early years is proportionate: promote respect and turn-taking, notice concerning language or family views that glorify harm, and report via the DSL. A four-year-old playing superheroes is not a Prevent referral.
  • Wellbeing is promoted through relationships, outdoor time, food, rest, and a climate where feelings can be named. Transitions (room moves, school, family change) should be planned. Mental health in the early years often shows as behaviour, sleep or play; respond with co-regulation and, where needed, the health visitor or other services via the usual partnership routes.

On placement

  • On day one, identify the DSL and deputy, find the concern form, and ask how quickly a concern must be recorded.
  • Complete a risk-benefit assessment for one outdoor experience using the five HSE steps, including your own safety.
  • Watch a safer-sleeping rest time and check the practice against the written policy (position, clear cot, checks).
  • Learn the allergy children in your room, where the care plans live, and what you must never give at snack.
  • Practise writing a factual accident record (no blame, no diagnosis) and compare it with a model in the setting's file.
  • Never use a personal phone in the room. If you see a colleague doing so, follow the setting's policy; that can be a safeguarding issue.
  • If something worries you about a child or an adult, tell the DSL the same day even if you feel unsure. Unsure and reported is professional; unsure and silent is not.

A parent has emailed to demand that water play and outdoor climbing are stopped. Staff are anxious. The manager has also reminded the team that a member of bank staff was seen using a personal phone in the baby room. You are the student asked to contribute to the team discussion.
